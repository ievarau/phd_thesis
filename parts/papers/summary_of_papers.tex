
\chapter{Summary of papers}

This chapter summarizes the three publications that are the foundation of this thesis. The unifying subject of this thesis is the investigation of transcription factor binding patterns and mechanisms. This subject was explored via two main aims: 1) new motif discovery and addition to the JASPAR database and 2) identification of co-binding patterns.

\section{Papers I-II. Contribution to the 2022 and 2024 releases of JASPAR database}

\textbf{\underline{Rauluseviciute I}}\footnotemark[\value{footnote}], Riudavets-Puig R\footnotemark[\value{footnote}], Blanc-Mathieu R, Castro-Mondragon JA, Ferenc K, Kumar V, Lemma RB, Lucas J, Chèneby J, Baranasic D, Khan A, Fornes O, Gundersen S, Johansen M, Hovig E, Lenhard B, Sandelin A, Wasserman WW, Parcy F, Mathelier A. \textit{JASPAR 2024: 20th anniversary of the open-access database of transcription factor binding profiles.} Nucleic Acids Research. 2024 Jan 5;52(D1):D174-82; doi: 10.1093/nar/gkad1059. \footnotetext{These authors should be regarded as joint first authors.}

\textbf{Paper I} and \textbf{Paper II} describe the two latest releases of the JASPAR database. JASPAR is a widely used open-access repository of transcription factor binding motifs created and maintained since 2004. One of the biggest database strengths is that all motifs are manually curated before they are added to the database. Motifs subjected for manual curation are discovered \textit{de novo} from peak data, mined from databases (for example, ReMap \cite{hammal2022remap} and GTRD \cite{kolmykov2021gtrd}) and publications, or already generated motifs are mined from such sources. Database is updated every 2 years by adding new profiles into two collections - CORE and UNVALIDATED. CORE collection contains profiles with orthogonal support, while UNVALIDATED collection has profiles of same good quality, but not yet confirmed by an independent experiment. In 2022 CORE and UNVALIDATED collections were enhanced or updated by 501 and 182 profiles, respectively, while in 2024 by 404 and 298 profiles, respectively.

Since 2022, JASPAR is not only updated with new information, but existing profiles in both collections are consistently revised. We revise UNVALIDATED collection profiles to check for possible new validation to move the profiles into the CORE collection. In 2022, we upgraded 52 such profiles, while in 2024 validation was found for 72 profiles. We also extensively revised the inconsistencies in the metadata that were accumulated over the years since the release of JASPAR. Metadata fields, such as TF class and family were revised and updated with the newest available information. For example, vertebrate profiles are classified using TFClass \cite{wingender2018tfclass}, while plant TF classification was extensively revised both in 2022 and 2024 and now is based on \cite{blanc2024plant}. 75\% (1,869 out of 2,506) JASPAR 2022 motifs and all incoming new motifs were updated by trimming. We applied an algorithm to remove the low IC positions on the sides of motifs to improve the quality of our collections.

In addition to the augmenting and refining the motifs and related metadata, we provide additional analysis and resources. This includes motif clustering, familial binding motifs, TFBSs predictions, genome browser tracks, word clouds. For each taxon we cluster CORE collection motifs individually and CORE and UNVALIDATED motifs together. Clusters are visualized as radial trees and annotated with TF class information. Furthermore, the motif clusters were used to construct familial binding profiles. Such motif represent what DNA binding preference has closely related TFs. Latest versions of CORE collection motifs are used to scan the most popular genomes to predict TFBSs, which are available to users along with the genome browser tracks. Finally, since 2022 word clouds are generated for each TF in JASPAR to illustrate their functional roles.

JASPAR database is user-friendly and can be accessed via a number of channels. Using command-line, RESTful API, R Bioconductor package and pyJASPAR. To accompany the data we also provide a number of computational tools. In the past two releases we added an enrichment analysis tool that allows to study the enrichment of the binding of a specific TF in a set of genomic regions, and a TFBS extraction tool for easier extraction of TFBSs in a genomic region of interest.

JASPAR database update process is complex, but We have refined it into three processes, abbreviated as "3D" - discovery, downstream, deployment. Each process has its own software component in a form of a workflow or a pipeline. In the discovery motifs are processed for manual curation. After manual curation, in the downstream analysis, curated motifs are prepared for the website and all additional analysis performed to produce the data needed for the website and other related sources. Finally, the deployment workflow collects all produced data and updates the website. All software components consistently receive updates to implement new features, address users' needs and increase the reliability and performance of the entire process of JASPAR update.

My contributions to this paper included data collection and processing, manual curation of the profiles. In the downstream analysis part I contributed to the software development, data analysis (emphasizing motif trimming) and preparation for the website.

\section{Paper III. Identification of transcription factor co-binding patterns with non-negative matrix factorization}

\textbf{\underline{Rauluseviciute I}}, Launay T, Barzaghi G, Nikumbh S, Lenhard B, Krebs AR, Castro-Mondragon JA, Mathelier A. \textit{Identification of transcription factor co-binding patterns with non-negative matrix factorization.} bioRxiv. 2023:2023-04.
\\

\textbf{Paper III} describes a non-negative matrix factorization-based method to identify co-binding DNA patterns. The tool COBIND can be used to identify DNA/RNA patterns in relation to the provided set of reference regions/sequences (anchors). In the paper, COBIND was applied on a collection of known TFBSs to automatically identify TF co-binding patterns in the regions flanking them. Interpreting such predicted DNA patterns can give insights about TF co-binding and a more complex gene regulation mechanism it creates.

In COBIND, NMF is applied to one-hot encoded sequences and pinpoints the enriched DNA patterns at fixed instances. We used 5,699 TFBS datasets from UniBind database for 401 TFs in seven species \cite{puig2021unibind}. As proof-of-concept, we present a classical example of POU5F1 co-binding with SOX2 or SOX17. For SOX2 and SOX17 TFBS datasets we were able to recover the DNA pattern that represents POU5F1 motif with different spacings, corresponding to canonical (SOX2::POU5F1 pair) and compressed (SOX17::POU5F1 pair) motifs. The binding of POU5F1 was inferred through motif similarity and TF pair presence in the protein-protein interaction (PPI) data.

All discovered co-binding patterns were evaluated with motif similarity and PPI analysis, where 78\% (462 out of 591) of the patterns had support in the PPI data. For example, TEAD1 co-binding with TEAD family members. However, a fraction of inferred pairs are not yet reported in the PPI data or did not have a good motif match. In \textit{A. thaliana} genome, the same co-binding pattern was found for HY5, ABF1, ABF3, ABF4, GBF2, and GBF3 anchors. Motif analysis revealed the similarity with the motif recognized by human NF-Y TFs (NF-YC and NF-YA). However, such interactions are not currently supported by the PPI data. Another example, where COBIND prediction was not supported by the PPI data, is a co-binding pattern of CTCF. The same co-binding pattern was found with two different spacings in \textit{H. sapiens}, \textit{M. musculus} and \textit{D. rerio} TFBS datasets. In the literature, we found that the predicted DNA pattern is not bound by another TF, but by CTCF itself. CTCF is a zinc-finger protein and in a fraction of its binding sites 9-11 zinc fingers binds to an extended portion of the motif.

We also demonstrated that the regions with predicted co-binding patterns are likely functionally relevant as they are more evolutionarily conserved compared to the regions that contain only the anchor TFBSs. Across species and all predictions we systematically compared the conservation at the locations of anchor and predicted co-binding sites. We observed that 23--83\% of the datasets from different species with predicted co-binding patterns showed a significantly higher conservation at the co-binding motif locations compared to the alternative locations in regions only with the anchor motifs. For anchor sites, 31--67\% of the datasets demonstrated a more conserved anchor site in the regions with predicted co-binding patterns. We observed for 23--64\% of datasets that the locations of both the anchor and a co-binding patterns were more conserved in the co-bound regions.

Furthermore, we investigated regions with and without predicted co-binders in the open chromatin data by analysing DNase I hypersensitivity (DHS) footprints from matching human cell-types. By performing a similar analysis to the conservation, we found that in 85\% of co-binding pattern - DHS dataset pairs, DHS footprints were significantly deeper at the locations of the co-binding patterns in regions with predicted co-binding motifs compared to the regions without (20\% was expected by chance). DHS footprints in anchor locations were significantly deeper in co-bound regions for 78\% of the pairs (21\% expected by chance). When considering deeper footprints for both anchor and co-binding pattern sites, significance was never observed in a random expectation, but with real data it was observed for 73\% of the pattern-dataset pairs.

Finally, single-molecule footprinting (SMF) data from mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) confirmed that the predicted co-binding events associated for a set of TFs are likely occurring on the same DNA molecules. We analysed predictions for 17 anchor TFs, where co-binding patterns were found in a total of 1,694 regions. For each of these genomic regions and regions with only anchor motifs, we determined proportions of molecules in five states: accessible, occupied by nucleosomes, only occupied at the anchor motif site, only occupied at the co-binding motif site or occupied at both sites. Overall, we found that a significantly larger fraction of molecules was co-occupied at the both sites in the regions with COBIND-predicted co-binding motifs compared to the regions only with the anchor motifs. When looking at the individual anchors, the significance was observed for 8 out of 17 analysed anchor TFs.

We introduced a computational framework COBIND (\url{https://bitbucket.org/CBGR/cobind_tool/src/main/}) for DNA motif pattern discovery \textit{de novo} in the vicinity of predetermined regions. During extensive analysis we produced a collection of predicted co-binding patterns in 7 species, which are provided to the community at \url{https://cbgr.bitbucket.io/COBIND_results_page/}. DNA patterns discovered in the fixed locations from known TFBSs reveal the possible cooperative events and can be studied to investigate more complex TF-DNA interactions.

My contributions to this paper included developing of the method, performing the analysis, preparing the tool and resources.
