\chapter{General background}

The genome is a blueprint of life. It codes a recipe for the growth, development of the cell, and regulation of all cellular processes. In multicellular organisms, such as humans, the genome encodes intricate intracellular interactions that enable a collection of cells to become one coordinated organism. \ac{DNA} composes the genome, and through the production of \ac{RNA} and proteins, complex cellular mechanisms organize all the biological processes. We hope to understand better how the genome functions by studying these mechanisms. This chapter overviews basic molecular biology concepts, such as DNA and transcription, to provide the background to dive deeper into how transcription is regulated.

\section{Organization of the genome}

In Eukaryotes, the cell nucleus stores hereditary information as DNA. Genomic DNA is transcribed and replicated. Human DNA is only 2 nanometers wide, but if we stretch it into a single thread, it would be around 2 meters long \cite{ross1999human}. The microscopic nucleus stores tightly compacted DNA. Multiple levels of the organization are applied, starting from DNA helix and histone proteins and ending with chromosomes and their territories in the nucleus ({Figure \ref{genome_organization}}) \cite{misteli2020self}. The specific organization ensures proper genome functioning --- expressing genes in the right place and time.

    \subsection{DNA}

    The existence of DNA was discovered in 1869 by Friedrich Miescher when he found a new substance inside the nuclei of the human white blood cells that did not have the same properties as proteins or lipids \cite{pray2008discovery,dahm2008discovering}. In 1953, contributions of Rosalind Franklin, James Watson, and Francis Crick led to the publication of resolved DNA molecule structure and is often considered the beginning of molecular biology \cite{watson1953molecular}. This fundamental moment in science history allowed us to explain the possible mechanism of DNA replication, thus information multiplication and transfer.

    DNA stores genetic information in the cell. In the DNA structure, the sugar backbone (deoxyribose) holds four main types of nucleotides: purines \ac{A} and \ac{G}, and pyrimidines \ac{T} and \ac{C} \cite{dna_structure} \cite{ross1999human}. Chargaff's rule states that in the DNA of any species, the proportions of A and T nucleotides and C and G are always equal, demonstrating unique base-pairing (complementarity), when forming a double-stranded DNA helix \cite{chargaff1951composition,dna_structure}. A-T and C-G nucleotide pairs in two strands of the DNA and are held by two and three hydrogen bonds, respectively ({Figure \ref{dna_structure}}).

    The most commonly found DNA structure is called a B form of DNA. Two helical DNA strands entwine to form spaces between the strands, major and minor grooves, which are 22 and 12 \AA{} wide, respectively. The grooves create space for the proteins to interact with the double-stranded DNA. It is a basis for transcription factor (TF) binding \cite{protein_dna_interaction}.

    \subsection{Chromatin}

    The basic element of chromatin is a nucleosome, which consists of 146 bp of DNA wrapped around the core histone protein octamer. Nucleosomes gather into a chromatin fiber typically 5-24 nanometers in diameter. The fibers are organized into higher structures and eventually into a metaphase chromosome. During interphase, chromatin is organized into domains and compartments and tends to localize in specific territories ({Figure \ref{genome_organization}}) \cite{misteli2020self,morrison2021molecular}. Chromatin fibers from different chromosomes interact at the edges but do not entangle. \ac{TADs} is a chromatin organization feature relevant for transcription regulation \cite{luppino2020cohesin}.

    Canonical core histone proteins are H2A, H2B, H3, and H4, with two copies each in a single octameric histone core. A linker histone H1 separates every core nucleosome \cite{marino2005histone,morrison2021molecular}. The variants in specific genomic regions replace well-conserved canonical histone proteins. For example, H3.3 replaces the H3 histone in active genes and promoter regions \cite{marino2005histone, talbert2021histone}. Histone protein N-terminal tails undergo extensive \ac{PTMs}, such as acetylation and methylation, on their amino acids. These modifications are essential for controlling the accessibility of the chromatin. Accessible chromatin is accessed by a transcriptional machinery and regulatory proteins \cite{marino2005histone}.

    Based on its activity state, chromatin can be heterochromatin or euchromatin \cite{karpinska2023role}. Euchromatin is a more open, active chromatin with more widely spaced nucleosomes. Several histone modifications (\ac{H3K4me3}, \ac{H3K27ac}, \ac{H3K36me}) and histone variants (H3.3, H2A.Z, H2A.B) are marks of active transcription. Heterochromatin is generally known as a silent chromatin and is less accessible and transcribed. Constitutive heterochromatin is more static and contains \ac{H3K9me3}. \ac{H3K27me3} marks the facultative heterochromatin, which can switch between active and inactive states. For example, facultative heterochromatin marks an inactive X-chromosome \cite{morrison2021molecular}.

\section{Gene transcription}

Gene is a basic unit of heredity. It is a stretch of DNA in the genome and is subjected to transcription by RNA polymerase. Transcription is a process of copying DNA and producing an RNA molecule. Genes can be protein-coding or non-coding. The protein-coding RNA template is translated into a protein, while non-coding genes produce non-coding RNA molecules. A collection of proteins assembles into transcriptional machinery and organizes the transcription process.

    \subsection{RNA}

    Transcription machinery, with the leading player RNA polymerase, transcribes a portion of the genome and produces RNA. RNA molecule is single-stranded and consists of the sugar backbone (ribonuclease) and four types of nucleotides: \ac{U}, \ac{A}, \ac{C} and \ac{G} ({Figure \ref{rna_structure}}). RNA molecules can adopt various secondary structures by forming hydrogen bonds between nucleotides (C pairs with G, while A pairs with U) within the same molecule \cite{higgs2000rna}.

    The main types of RNAs, based on their functions, are \ac{mRNA}, \ac{rRNA}, \ac{tRNA}, and \ac{ncRNAs} \cite{mrna,raina2014trnas,higgs2000rna}. A \ac{pre-mRNA} is produced in the nucleus during transcription by copying a gene ({Figure \ref{gene_to_protein}}) \cite{mrna}. RNA polymerase synthesizes RNA from \ac{NTPs} (ATP, GTP, CTP, and UTP, precursors of A, G, C, and U, respectively). A pre-mRNA is heavily processed to produce an mRNA that carries information to the cytoplasm, where ribosomes translate it to a polypeptide. mRNA has an \ac{ORF}, consisted of introns and exons. The ORF is flanked by \ac{3'UTR} and \ac{5'UTR} \cite{moore2005birth,higgs2000rna}. During splicing, a specific protein complex, spliceosome, processes most of the pre-mRNA by cutting out intronic (non-protein coding) gene segments to leave only exons (protein-coding) \cite{lee2015mechanisms}. Introns can be spliced differently by choosing to leave out some exons, creating a variety of transcripts copied from the same gene. This process is known as alternative splicing \cite{faustino2003pre}. Splicing factor proteins recognize splicing signaling sequences at the intron-exon boundaries \cite{lee2015mechanisms,faustino2003pre}. In addition to the splicing, a cap is added at the 5' end of the RNA, while multiple added A nucleotides at the 3' end form a poly-A tail \cite{faustino2003pre}. After this, mRNA matures and travels from the nucleus to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm. Ribosomes receive individual mRNAs and read the information encoded. Ribosomes contain rRNA, and each amino acid is brought into the ribosome by tRNAs that recognize every three mRNA nucleotides, termed codons \cite{raina2014trnas,higgs2000rna}. Codons code for amino acids.

    Longer than 200 nucleotides non-coding RNAs (\ac{lncRNAs}) and shorter small ncRNAs are the two main size-based categories of ncRNAs. Small ncRNAs can be divided into \ac{miRNAs}, \ac{snoRNAs}, \ac{snRNAs}, \ac{siRNAs} and \ac{piRNAs}. miRNAs have an essential function in eukaryotic gene regulation. They can target and silence mRNAs to prevent translating them into proteins \cite{mrna}. Aberrations of the ncRNA activities can be associated with diseases such as cancer. For example, miRNA can act as a tumor suppressor and target genes responsible for cell growth.

    \subsection{RNA polymerase and transcriptional machinery}

    In eukaryotes, \ac{Pol II} is responsible for transcribing genes encoding mRNAs and some ncRNAs \cite{nikolov1997rna}. This twelve-subunit protein uses NTPs as a substrate to produce pre-mRNA molecules from a DNA template. The RNA sequence synthesis process follows nucleotide complementarity. In the DNA template, nucleotides A, C, G, and T will be transcribed into RNA nucleotides U, G, C, and A, respectively.

    Pol II is not able to recognize the \ac{TSSs} by itself and needs to be guided by a set of proteins known as \ac{GTFs} ({Figure \ref{gene_to_protein}}). Pol II and GTFs form a minimal transcription machinery, and all these proteins, together with the template DNA, form the \ac{PIC} \cite{liu2013rna,nikolov1997rna}. The GTFs are TFIIB, TFIID, TFIIE, TFIIF, and TFIIH, and each plays a specific role in transcription initiation. They assemble one after another. Proteins \ac{TBP} and \ac{TAFs} compose TFIID and recognize a particular sequence of DNA near the TSS, called a TATA box. TFIIB and the non-phosphorylated form of Pol II join the complex, followed by TFIIF, TFIIE, and TFIIH. Finally, Pol II is phosphorylated to proceed to elongation \cite{nikolov1997rna}. The transcriptional machinery produces a central portion of mRNA during transcription elongation. Transcription ends with the transcription termination process, and pre-mRNA travels for further processing and splicing.

    Different cell types at constant rates express a limited set of genes called housekeeping genes. However, for cells to differentiate and perform cell-type specific functions, specific genes need to be transcribed at a particular time and place. Basic transcriptional machinery cannot decide what genes to express, but various regulatory processes make those instructions. For example, transcription factors (TFs) bind regulatory regions, such as promoters and enhancers, and signal the PIC assembly at the associated gene promoter. Mechanisms of TF binding to the DNA and transcriptional regulation are very complex, and the field still lacks details on the organization of these processes.
