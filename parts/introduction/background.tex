\chapter{Background}

The genome is a blueprint of life. It codes a recipe for growth, development and regulation of all processes in the cell whether it is a multicellular or a single-cell organism. In multicellular organisms, such as humans, the genome dictates intricate intracellular interactions that enables a collection of cells to become one coordinated organism. The genome is composed of \ac{DNA} and through the production or proteins and RNA, complex cellular mechanisms are organizing all the biological processes. By studying these mechanisms, we hope to better understand how genome is functioning.

\section{Organization of the genome}

In Eukaryotes hereditary information is stored as DNA in the cell nucleus. Genomic DNA is transcribed and replicated. For example, if stretched into a single thread, human DNA is around 2 meters long and must be compacted into a microscopic nucleus. Therefore, multiple levels of organization are applied, starting from DNA helix and histone proteins and ending with chromosomes and their territories in the nucleus ({Figure \ref{genome_organization}}) \cite{misteli2020self}. Specific organization ensures proper functioning of the genome --- genes can be expressed in the right place and time.

    \subsection{DNA}

    The existence of DNA was discovered in 1869 by Friedrich Miescher when he found a new substance inside the nuclei of the human white blood cells that did not have the same properties as proteins or lipids \cite{pray2008discovery,dahm2008discovering}. Interestingly, this is not the discovery related to DNA that is most remembered. In 1953 contributions of Rosalind Franklin, James Watson and Francis Crick led to a publication of resolved DNA molecule structure and is often considered to be the beginning of molecular biology \cite{watson1953molecular}. This is a fundamental moment in science history as it allowed to explain the possible mechanism of DNA replication, thus information multiplication and transfer.

    DNA stores genetic information in the cell. Its structure is based on a sugar backbone (deoxyribose) that holds four main types of nucleotides --- purines \ac{A} and \ac{G}, and pyrimidines \ac{T} and \ac{C} \cite{dna_structure}. Chargaff's rule states that in the DNA of any species, the proportions of A and T nucleotides and C and G are always equal, demonstrating unique base-pairing (complementarity), when forming a double-stranded DNA helix \cite{chargaff1951composition,dna_structure}. A-T and C-G nucleotide pairs in two strands of the DNA are held by two and three hydrogen bonds respectively ({Figure \ref{dna_structure}}).

    The most commonly found DNA structure is called a B form DNA. Two helical DNA strands are entwined in a way that forms spaces between the strands --- major and minor grooves, which are 22 and 12 \AA wide, respectively. The grooves create opportunities for the proteins to interact with the double-stranded DNA, which is a basis for transcription factor (TF) binding \cite{protein_dna_interaction}.

    \subsection{Chromatin}

    The basic element of chromatin is a nucleosome, which consists of 146 bp of DNA wrapped around core histone protein octamer. Nucleosomes are gathered into a chromatin fibre, which is typically 5-24 nM in diameter. The fibres are organizing into higher structures and eventually into a metaphase chromosome. During interphase, chromatin is organized into domains and compartments and tends to be located in specific territories ({Figure \ref{genome_organization}}) \cite{misteli2020self,morrison2021molecular}. Chromatin fibres from different chromosomes can interact at the edges, but they do not entangle. \ac{TADs} is a chromatin feature relevant for gene expression control.

    Canonical core histone proteins are H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 with two copies of each in a single octameric core. Every core nucleosome is separated by a linker histone H1 \cite{marino2005histone,morrison2021molecular}. In certain genomic regions, well-conserved canonical histone proteins can be replaced by the variants. For example, H3.3 replaces the H3 histone in active genes and promoter regions \cite{marino2005histone, talbert2021histone}. Amino acids in histone protein N-terminal tails are subjected for extensive \ac{PTMs}, such as acetylation and methylation. These modifications are essential for controlling the accessibility of the chromatin. Accessible chromatin is accessed by a transcriptional machinery and regulatory proteins \cite{marino2005histone}.

    Based on its activity state chromatin can be grouped into heterochromatin and euchromatin. Euchromatin is a more open, active chromatin, where nucleosomes are more widely spaced. There are more histone modifications (\ac{H3K4me3}, \ac{H3K27ac}, \ac{H3K36me}) and histone variants (H3.3, H2A.Z, H2A.B) associated with active transcription. On the other hand, heterochromatin is generally known as a silent chromatin and is less accessible and transcribed. Constitutive heterochromatin is more static and contains \ac{H3K9me3}. Facultative heterochromatin can switch between active and inactive states and is marked by \ac{H3K27me3}. For example, facultative heterochromatin is found on an inactive X-chromosome \cite{morrison2021molecular}.

\section{Gene transcription}

Gene is a basic unit of heredity. It is a stretch of DNA in the genome and subjected for transcription by RNA polymerase. Transcription is a process of copying DNA and producing an RNA molecule. Genes can be protein-coding or non-coding. The RNA made using protein-coding gene as a template will be translated into a protein, while non-coding genes produce functional non-coding RNA molecules. The process of transcription is organized by a collection of proteins that assemble into transcriptional machinery.

    \subsection{RNA}

    RNA is produced during transcription, when transcription machinery with the main player RNA polymerase transcribes a portion of the genome. RNA molecule is single-stranded and similarly to the DNA, also consists of the sugar backbone (ribonuclease) and four types nucleotides --- \ac{U}, \ac{A}, \ac{C} and \ac{G} ({Figure \ref{rna_structure}}). RNA molecules can adapt a big variety of secondary structures via forming hydrogen bonds between nucleotides (C pairs with G, while A pairs with U) within the same molecule \cite{higgs2000rna}.

    Besides the structural variety, RNAs can be grouped by their functions. The main types of RNAs are \ac{mRNA}, \ac{rRNA}, \ac{tRNA} and broad category of other \ac{ncRNAs} \cite{mrna,raina2014trnas,higgs2000rna}. A \ac{pre-mRNA} is produced in the nucleus during transcription by copying a gene ({Figure \ref{gene_to_protein}}) \cite{mrna}. RNA is synthesized by RNA polymerase from \ac{NTPs} (ATP, GTP, CTP and UTP that are precursors of A, G, C and U, respectively). They are heavily processed to produce an mRNA that carries information to the cytoplasm to be translated to polypeptide. mRNA has an \ac{ORF}, consisted of introns and exons. The ORF is flanked by \ac{3'UTR} and \ac{5'UTR} \cite{moore2005birth,higgs2000rna}. Most of the pre-mRNA is processed by splicing, where intronic (non-protein coding) gene segments are cut out to leave only exons (protein-coding) \cite{lee2015mechanisms}. Introns can be spliced differently by choosing to leave out some exons, creating a variety of transcripts copied from the same gene. This is known as alternative splicing \cite{faustino2003pre}. A specific protein complex, spliceosome, is responsible for splicing. Splicing factor proteins recognize splicing signalling sequences at the intron-exon boundaries \cite{lee2015mechanisms,faustino2003pre}. In addition to the splicing, a cap is added at the 5' end of the RNA, while to the 3' end multiple A nucleotides are added to create a poly-A tail \cite{faustino2003pre}. After this, mRNA is mature and travels out of the nucleus to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm. Ribosomes receive individual mRNAs and reads the information encoded. Ribosomes contain rRNA and each amino acid is brought into the ribosome by tRNAs that recognize every three mRNA nucleotides, termed codons \cite{raina2014trnas,higgs2000rna}.

    Non-coding RNAs can be classified into two subcategories based on their size --- \ac{lncRNAs} (longer than 200 nucleotides) and small ncRNAs (shorter than 200 nucleotides). The later group can be divided into \ac{miRNAs}, \ac{snoRNAs}, \ac{snRNAs}, \ac{siRNAs} and \ac{piRNAs}. miRNAs have an important function in eukaryotic gene regulation. They can target and silence mRNAs to prevent translating them into proteins \cite{mrna}. Many ncRNAs have been associated with diseases, such as cancer. For example, miRNA can act as a tumour suppressor and target genes responsible for cell growth. Aberrations in such miRNA functions can lead to tumorigenesis.

    \subsection{RNA polymerase and transcriptional machinery}

    In eukaryotes, \ac{Pol II} is responsible for transcribing genes encoding mRNAs and some ncRNAs  \cite{nikolov1997rna}. This 12 subunit protein uses NTPs as a substrate to produce pre-mRNA molecules from a DNA template. RNA sequence is synthesized by using nucleotide complementarity. In the DNA template nucleotides A, C, G and T will be transcribed into RNA nucleotides U, G, C and A, respectively.
    
    However, Pol II is not able to recognize the \ac{TSSs} by itself and needs to be guided by a set of proteins, known as \ac{GTFs} ({Figure \ref{gene_to_protein}}). Pol II and GTFs forms a minimal transcription machinery and all these proteins together with the template DNA form the \ac{PIC} \cite{liu2013rna,nikolov1997rna}. The GTFs are TFIIB, TFIID, TFIIE, TFIIF and TFIIH, and each of them play a specific role in transcription initiation. They assemble one after another. TFIID is composed of \ac{TBP} and \ac{TAFs}, and recognizes a specific DNA sequence element near the TSS, TATA box. TFIIB and non-phosphorylated form of Pol II joins the complex, followed by TFIIF, TFIIE and TFIIH. Finally, Pol II is phosphorylated to proceed to elongation \cite{nikolov1997rna}. During transcription elongation the main portion of mRNA is produced. Transcription ends with transcription termination process, where pre-mRNA is released for further processing, splicing.
