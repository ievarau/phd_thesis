\chapter{Background}

The genome is a blueprint of life. It codes a recipe for growth, development, regulation of all processes in the cell whether it is a multicellular or a single-cell organism. In multicellular organisms, such as humans, the genome dictates intricate intracellular interactions that enables a collection of cells to become one coordinated organism. The genome is composed of \ac{DNA} and through the production or proteins and RNA, complex cellular mechanisms are organizing all the biological processes. By studying these mechanisms, we hope to better understand how genome is functioning.

\section{Organization of the genome}

In Eukaryotes hereditary information is stored as DNA in the cell nucleus. Genomic DNA is transcribed and replicated. For example, if stretched into a single thread, human DNA is around 2 meters long and must be compacted into a microscopic nucleus. Therefore, multiple levels of organization are applied, starting from DNA helix and histone proteins and ending with chromosomes and their territories in the nucleus ({Figure \ref{genome_organization}}) \cite{misteli2020self}. Specific organization ensures proper functioning of the genome --- genes can be expressed in the right place and time.

    \subsection{DNA}

    The existence of DNA was discovered in 1869 by Friedrich Miescher when he found a new substance inside the nuclei of the human white blood cells that did not have the same properties as proteins or lipids \cite{pray2008discovery,dahm2008discovering}. Interestingly, this is not the discovery related to DNA that is most remembered. In 1953 contributions of Rosalind Franklin, James Watson and Francis Crick led to a publication of resolved DNA molecule structure and is often considered to be the beginning of molecular biology \cite{watson1953molecular}. This is a fundamental moment in science history as it allowed to explain the possible mechanism of DNA replication, thus information multiplication and transfer.

    DNA stores genetic information in the cell. Its structure is based on a sugar backbone (deoxyribose) that holds four main types of nucleotides --- purines \ac{A} and \ac{G}, and pyrimidines \ac{T} and \ac{C} \cite{dna_structure}. Chargaff's rule states that in the DNA of any species, the proportions of A and T nucleotides and C and G are always equal, demonstrating unique base-pairing (complementarity), when forming a double-stranded DNA helix \cite{chargaff1951composition,dna_structure}. A-T and C-G nucleotide pairs in two strands of the DNA are held by two and three hydrogen bonds respectively ({Figure \ref{dna_structure}}).

    The most commonly found DNA structure is called a B form DNA. Two helical DNA strands are entwined in a way that forms spaces between the strands --- major and minor grooves, which are 22 and 12 \AA wide, respectively. The grooves create opportunities for the proteins to interact with the double-stranded DNA, which is a basis for transcription factor (TF) binding \cite{protein_dna_interaction}.
