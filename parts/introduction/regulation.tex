\chapter{Transcription regulation}

Multicellular organisms, such as humans, are formed by thousands of cell types. However, the genome in every one of those cells is identical. If the recipe is the same, how is it possible to have so many cell types that manage to work together and run such a complex organism? The Human Genome project expected that once we have a complete sequence of the genome, it will be fairly straightforward to solve the causes of diseases, and easier to understand human evolution and development. But as it turned out, the majority of the human genome (98\%) does not code for proteins but is essential for the regulation of all expressed genes \cite{pang2023identification}. The genome is much more mysterious, and we are still studying its complexity \cite{kumar2016human}.

Processes that are not directly associated with the DNA sequence, are responsible for controlling the genome and its gene expression. Such mechanisms are termed epigenetic mechanisms (epi- means "over, outside") \cite{jablonka2002changing}. DNA methylation, histone tail modifications, and micro RNA are all contributing to the regulation of gene expression transcription regulation \cite{mitsis2020transcription}. Genomic DNA is constantly being approached by regulatory proteins and other small molecules. The major players in transcriptional regulation are \ac{TFs}. Without TFs RNA polymerase and the basic transcriptional machinery would not be able to approach genes and begin transcription. TFs act in different modes and can find and bind regulatory regions --- promoters and enhancers. Bound TFs attract Mediator proteins and Pol II and form a loop that brings enhancers and promoter regions close in space even though they can be separated by kilobases in a linear space ({Figure \ref{regions_loop}}). The activation of gene expression is tightly regulated and is cell type- and time-specific.

  \section{Cis-regulatory regions}

  Regulatory regions in the eukaryotic genome are segments of the DNA that are associated with the capability to increase or decrease transcription. These regions are termed \ac{CRRs}. They are crucial for gene expression regulation because they contain a set of short DNA sequences, \ac{TFBSs}, which are targeted by TFs \cite{wasserman2004applied}. CRRs include promoters, enhancers, and silencers.

    \subsection{Promoters}

    Promoters are located at the 5' end region of the gene ({Figure \ref{regions_loop}}A) and are crucial for gene expression regulation. Core promoters are located 40 bp upstream or downstream from the transcription start site (TSS) and are minimal regions needed for transcription initiation \cite{haberle2016promoter,kadonaga2012perspectives}. Promoters have specific DNA sequence elements important for gene regulation. For example, the TATA box element in the core promoter is bound by TBP, the Initiator sequence is one of the most common elements and encompasses the +1 TSS, while different sequence motifs can be bound by specific TFs \cite{villao2023plant,wasserman2004applied,haberle2016promoter,kadonaga2012perspectives}. Another characteristic common for human promoters is the abundance of CpG dinucleotides. CpGs are targets for cytosine methylation, which is one of the gene regulatory mechanisms \cite{wasserman2004applied,saxonov2006genome}. Around 70\% of human promoters near TSSs contain a \ac{CGI}, which is a region with a high frequency of CpGs \cite{deaton2011cpg,saxonov2006genome,haberle2016promoter}.

    Based on promoter composition, nucleosome positioning, and what genes they regulate, promoters can be classified into four main types: type I, II, III, and IV \cite{haberle2016promoter}. Type I promoters are adult tissue-specific and their transcription starts at the precise position. These promoters have a TATA box around 30 bp upstream from the TSS and nucleosomes are distributed in a disordered fashion along the sequence. Type II promoters control the expression of the housekeeping genes and transcription can be initiated at several locations. Type III promoters regulate developmental genes and also have a broad initiation pattern. Interestingly, these promoters depend on the input from distal enhancers. Type IV promoters are transcriptional machinery-specific and, like type I, have a sharp transcription initiation pattern. In type II, III, and IV promoters there is a nucleosome-free region with precisely positioned two nucleosomes downstream and upstream. In addition, all three types of promoters have CGIs covering the TSSs \cite{haberle2016promoter}.

    \subsection{Enhancers and silencers}

    Enhancers are distal regulatory regions that activate transcription by acting on a promoter over large genomic distances ({Figure \ref{regions_loop}}) \cite{smith2014enhancer}. The distance from the enhancer to the promoter it acts on can vary from several kilobases to megabases \cite{field2020evaluating,karpinska2023role}. The size of enhancers also varies from less than 100 bp long elements to 10--100 kb sequences, which are sometimes referred to as super-enhancers \cite{field2020evaluating,hnisz2013super,smith2014enhancer}. They often act in a spatially and temporally restricted manner, which means that enhancers are cell-type specific and are active only at specific time points \cite{field2020evaluating}. Enhancers are approached by transcription factors and bind specific DNA patterns. Multiple TFs can bind one enhancer, depending on how many potential TFBSs that enhancer has \cite{wu2019super,field2020evaluating}. Fairly recently it has been discovered that enhancer DNA is being transcribed into non-coding RNA, termed \ac{eRNA} ({Figure \ref{regions_loop}}B) \cite{smith2014enhancer}. These RNAs promote the functions of enhancers by recruiting TFs, promoting enhancer-promoter looping, and influencing chromatin conformation or histone acetylation status \cite{wu2019super,field2020evaluating}.

    Enhancer-promoter looping occurs in isolated chromatin structures - loops and \ac{TADs} ({Figure \ref{loops_tads}}). Cohesin is a ring-like structure that holds the chromatin loops together letting enhancer and promoter communicate through complexes of proteins \cite{smith2014enhancer,field2020evaluating,davidson2021genome}. The formation of loops is known as loop extrusion. Another loop-extruding factor is protein CTCF. It binds DNA at the TAD boundaries, this way informing where to stop the loop extrusion process closing the entire domain ({Figure \ref{loops_tads}}A) \cite{davidson2021genome}. Multiple loops held or not by cohesin can be found in one TAD \cite{karpinska2023role,luppino2020cohesin,davidson2021genome} ({Figure \ref{loops_tads}}B-C). CTCF binding motif orientation is important for the loop extrusion since two CTCF proteins bound convergently are required to close the loop \cite{davidson2021genome}. It is described as insulating a particular region \cite{field2020evaluating}. The influence of the enhancers is most of the time, but not always, limited to the genes that are within the same loop \cite{karpinska2023role,luppino2020cohesin,symmons2014functional}. One enhancer can regulate multiple genes, while one gene can be regulated by multiple enhancers.

    Opposite to the enhancers, silencers are DNA sequences that are bound by the repressor proteins to silence gene transcription \cite{spitz2012transcription}. Even though most of the research is focused on enhancer and promoter identification, silencers are interesting and important regions to research. It is known that silencers can also be tissue-specific and that they operate within TADs through short- or long-range interactions \cite{pang2020systematic,pang2023identification}. These regions are marked by specific chromatin marks, such as \ac{H4K20me} \cite{pang2020systematic,beck2012pr}. Silencers are targeted by repressive TFs and through looping communicate with the promoter of the gene, whose expression needs to be repressed \cite{pang2023identification}.

  \section{Transcription factors}

  Transcription factors (TFs) are key regulators of transcription \cite{mitsis2020transcription,weidemuller2021transcription}. About 10\% of the human protein-coding genome codes for transcription factors \cite{levine2003transcription}. TFs act as a bridge between the cell signaling pathways and gene expression. Through receptors, external cues trigger post-translational modification cascades that eventually result in the modulation of a set of TFs, which are then recruited at the gene regulatory regions \cite{weidemuller2021transcription}. Which TFs are modified depends on the stimuli, cell type, and state. In this section TFs that directly interpret the genome by binding to the specific DNA sequence patterns will be discussed. Short DNA sequences recognized by TFs are known as DNA motifs. The function of TFs is often studied together with the DNA motifs that they recognize \cite{lambert2018human}. There are various experimental methods to identify TF-DNA interactions and a variety of computational tools to model and represent those interactions. Most of the experimental techniques are limited to investigating individual TFs. However, in reality, TFs often cooperate, compete for the same binding site, or interact with the DNA indirectly \cite{mitsis2020transcription}. All these are complex TF activity mechanisms that are still difficult to investigate and interpret with the currently available tools.

    \subsection{Protein structures and classification}

    Eukaryotic transcription factor proteins have at least two domains, one for DNA binding and one for activation or repression. Optionally there are \ac{PPI} or effector domains to regulate TFs activity \cite{zhu2011mapping,minchin201393,wingender2013criteria}. \ac{DBD} recognize and bind DNA motifs. Thousands of protein-DNA structures are already solved and available in resources, such as \ac{PDB} \cite{yesudhas2017proteins,berman2000protein,wingender2013criteria}. For example, the \ac{bHLH} domain proteins have two \( \alpha \)-helices connected by a turn. These domains are known to dimerize, and each monomer recognizes one side of a symmetric DNA sequence ({Figure \ref{dbd_classification}}A). bHLH proteins bind at the major groove of the DNA molecule \cite{yesudhas2017proteins}. Homeodomain-type DBDs are highly conserved. POU5F1, also known as OCT4, has two DBDs, which are binding cooperatively at the major groove ({Figure \ref{dbd_classification}}B).

    DBDs are extremely well-conserved and used to classify TFs into families and classes, where proteins with a very similar amino acid sequence in the DBD are grouped. It is known that TFs from the same class or family tend to recognize the same or highly similar DNA patterns ({Figure \ref{dbd_classification}}C) \cite{mitsis2020transcription}. This is an important feature that could help to define binding specificities of understudied TFs \cite{wingender2013criteria}. In addition, DBD can provide information about the protein function. For example, homeodomain TFs are often involved in developmental processes \cite{vaquerizas2009census}. In 2009 23 human TF families were defined, where 80\% of all known human TFs at the time had C\textsubscript{2}H\textsubscript{2} zinc-finger, homeodomain, or HLH DBDs \cite{vaquerizas2009census}. Later the classification was updated several times, also expanding from humans to all mammalian TFs. One of the most extensive classifications of mammalian transcription factors, TFClass, includes superclass, class, family, subfamily, and genus categories \cite{wingender2018tfclass, wingender2013criteria,wingender2015tfclass}. TF families and class categories are the most used to annotate TFs, for example, in the JASPAR database. The most up-to-date version classifies more than 39,000 TFs from up to 41 mammalian species. TF DBDs were annotated and TFs were classified using phylogenetic analyses \cite{wingender2018tfclass}. More recently, a similar classification of plant TFs was released. Many DBDs present in plants, but not found in mammals were hindering the full classification of the plant TFs. Plant-TFClass addressed that by adding 8 new classes and 37 new families to classify most of the 56 plant TF types \cite{blanc2024plant}. This recent development in TF classification highlights the need to consistently revise and update current classifications, considering other taxa, such as nematodes or insects.

  \subsection{Mechanisms of transcription regulation by TFs}

    \subsubsection{Pioneering}

    A specific group of transcription factors can interact with the nucleosome-protected closed chromatin and trigger its opening for other TFs and proteins \cite{barral2023pioneer}. These TFs are known as pioneer transcription factors. FOXA and GATA4 were the first factors detected to engage the enhancers in development and their occupancy preceded occupancy of the other TFs that bind to those enhancers \cite{zaret2011pioneer,cirillo2002opening,yu2020pioneer}. Today, well-characterized pioneer TFs include SOX2, KLF4, and OCT4, which are also known as Yamanaka factors because they can be used to induce pluripotency \cite{yu2020pioneer}. It was shown that pioneers bind the condensed chromatin before the activation of transcription, therefore showing their essentiality for region transcriptional activation \cite{yu2020pioneer}. It is known that in some cases (p53) (SOX2, KLF4, and OCT4) it is enough for a pioneer TF to recognize a partial DNA motif to bind closed chromatin, in other scenarios pioneers prefer binding near the nucleosome edge and their binding affinity is stronger than to the sites located in the naked DNA \cite{yu2020pioneer}. After binding, pioneer TFs open closed chromatin locally first. Then ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers, such as the SWI/SNF remodeling complex, are recruited to promote nucleosome eviction in the region \cite{barral2023pioneer}. Finally, acetyltransferases are recruited to modify histone tails and activate the cis-regulatory element \cite{barral2023pioneer,boeva2016analysis}.

    \subsubsection{Impact of methylation to the DNA binding}

    DNA modifications impact how transcription factors bind \cite{wang2019identification}. The most extensively studied epigenetic DNA modification is DNA methylation. Cytosines in the DNA molecule are modified by adding a methyl group in the context of CpG dinucleotides, generating \ac{5mC} ({Figure \ref{tf_methylation_binding}}A). This reaction is performed by "writers" --- \ac{DNMTs}, and the removal of methyl groups is conducted by \ac{TET} enzymes \cite{wang2019identification,schubeler2015function}. Generally, hypermethylation of CpGs in the promoter regions is associated with transcription repression, while gene body hypermethylation indicates active genes \cite{schubeler2015function,ball2009targeted}. Therefore, DNA methylation is very important for TFs that bind methylated motifs, and it has been shown that some prefer to interact with methylated sites or, in contrast, are prevented from binding to methylated motifs \cite{yin2017impact,schubeler2015function}. For example, the HOXB13 protein has three key residues that contribute to the preferential mCpG binding ({Figure \ref{tf_methylation_binding}}B-C) \cite{yin2017impact}. HOXB13 can also bind to the unmethylated motif, which then does not contain a CpG dinucleotide ({Figure \ref{tf_methylation_binding}}C). A portion of TFs (for example, MAX) are not influenced by methylation even though their motifs contain a CpG \cite{yin2017impact}. Changes in the methylation patterns can lead to aberrant TF binding, affecting transcription regulation and possibly causing diseases.

    However, the way TF-DNA interactions are most commonly modeled today does not take into account methylated CpGs and position interdependency, which is of special relevance when analyzing CpG dinucleotides (read more about computational modeling of TF-DNA interactions in the next section). The presence of a CpG in the binding motif does not inform whether it needs to be methylated for the TF to bind. There are special attempts to model TF binding to the methylated DNA. For example, the MEDEMO toolkit analyzes TF binding motifs by including information about DNA methylation \cite{grau2023widespread}. The simplest way to include methylation information is to extend the alphabet where 5mC is replaced with M and G on the opposite strand with an H. Such methylation-aware genome can then be used to train methylation-aware TF binding models. These can therefore inform about methylation sensitivity ({Figure \ref{tf_methylation_binding}}D) and allow the identification of differential TF binding regions in ChIP-seq data \cite{grau2023widespread}.

  \subsection{Experimental methods to detect TF-DNA interactions}

  Over the years, multiple experimental methods were developed to detect the binding preferences of the transcription factors. Broadly these methods can be categorized into \textit{in vitro} and \textit{in vitro}. The majority of the methods are limited to investigating the interactions of a single TF at the time. The most popular and relevant methods in these categories will be briefly described in this section.

    \subsubsection{\textit{In vitro}}

    \textit{In vitro} methods mainly aim to identify TF binding sequence preferences. For example, \ac{SELEX} or PBM. Other methods, such as EMSA, can inform about the binding affinity \cite{geertz2010experimental}. The biggest limitation of \textit{in vitro} methods is that by design they are not able to give information about the biological context of the TF-DNA interaction.

    SELEX is a qualitative method developed decades ago, but high-throughput (HT-SELEX) and other versions are extensively used today \cite{kinzler1990gene,jolma2010multiplexed}. The basic principle of SELEX is the incubation of a purified TF with a pool of random DNA oligonucleotides ({Figure \ref{chip_selex}}A). Random sequences bound to the protein are collected, amplified, and incubated with the TF again. After multiple cycles of incubation, separation, elution, and amplification, the sequences that have the highest affinity are selected and sent to the sequencing procedure. The resulting sequencing data is analyzed to determine the TF binding sequence preference \cite{geertz2010experimental,jolma2010multiplexed}. Differently from a regular SELEX, in HT-SELEX one can study multiple transcription factors simultaneously. However, since the oligonucleotides are random, in SELEX it is not guaranteed that the sequences bound with the highest affinity reflect the functional binding preferences in a living cell.

    One variation of the SELEX protocol is of particular interest because it allows studying dimer TF sequence preferences. In the \ac{CAP-SELEX} protocol oligonucleotides are incubated with two TFs that are flagged with different flags. During two consecutive affinity purification steps, only those sequences bound by both TFs are selected for amplification \cite{jolma2015dna}. This technique gives an insight into how specifically two TFs can bind the same sequence. For example, we can determine preferred spacings or overlaps between two individual motifs and motif orientation \cite{jolma2015dna}.

    A high-throughput technique that could be classified in between \textit{in vitro} and \textit{in vivo} is called \ac{DAP-seq}. In DAP-seq, the libraries of sequences are constructed using native DNA, preserving cell-specific modifications. This provides increased specificity compared to methods, such as SELEX. The library of sequences is then incubated with \textit{in vitro}-expressed and tagged TF. TF-bound sequences are purified and sequenced. Finally, sequences are then mapped to the genome of origin to identify locations preferentially bound by a TF \cite{bartlett2017mapping}.

    \subsubsection{\textit{In vivo}}

    Arguably the most popular technique to map TF-DNA interactions in living cells is \ac{ChIP-seq} \cite{johnson2007genome}. The ChIP-seq process starts with cross-linking (using formaldehyde) of the DNA and proteins in the living cells ({Figure \ref{chip_selex}}B). Then DNA is randomly fragmented using sonication leaving DNA segments bound and unbound by proteins. In a crucial step, a specific antibody is used to bind the TF of interest. TF-DNA complexes bound by an antibody are selected by washing away everything else. Protein-DNA cross-links are reversed and DNA fragments, previously protected by the TF, are purified. These DNA sequences are then subjected to sequencing. After sequencing the data is analyzed by mapping the sequencing reads to a genome and identifying those genomic regions that are enriched by TF binding \cite{johnson2007genome,park2009chip}.

    The biggest advantage of \textit{in vivo} techniques, such as ChIP-seq, is that the TF binding locations are determined in a specific tissue or cell line, thus reflecting the TF binding preferences in physiological conditions \cite{park2009chip}. However, this comes with a few limitations. TF binding and regulatory mechanisms are complex, so \textit{in vivo} binding is influenced by several factors that affect the outcome of the experiment. Differences in TF-DNA affinity, protein-protein interactions, indirect binding, and sequence biases can all affect how results are interpreted \cite{jolma2010multiplexed,rhee2011comprehensive,rossi2018simplified}. Other limitations of the ChIP-seq technology include studying only one TF at a time and that the immunoprecipitation requires a high-quality antibody, which can be difficult to acquire if the protein of interest is relatively unknown or poorly annotated \cite{park2009chip}.

    In ChIP-seq, the DNA fragment sizes are variable and are longer (hundreds of base pairs) than the actual TF-protected region, because DNA is sheared randomly \cite{welch2017data,rhee2011comprehensive}. Therefore, the TF binding sites are not inferred in base pair resolution. Computational analysis and motif search are insufficient to identify all binding locations in ChIP-seq derived regions \cite{rhee2011comprehensive,yeh2022chip}. To overcome this limitation ChIP-exo technique was developed. The protocol has an additional step of immunoprecipitate treatment by 5` to 3` lambda exonuclease, which digests the unprotected ends of the DNA fragment bound by a TF. This allows us to get precise, nearly single-base resolution, genomic locations bound by a TF \cite{rhee2011comprehensive,yeh2022chip}. The length of such regions is 36-100 bp \cite{welch2017data}. A simplified version of the ChIP-exo protocol, ChIP-nexus, was developed in 2015 \cite{rossi2018simplified,welch2017data}.

    The latest methods for TF binding region identification \textit{in vivo} include CUT\&Tag and CUT\&RUN \cite{kaya2019cut}. Both techniques are similar and are based on specific antibodies recognizing the TF of interest. In CUT\&Tag, the antibody tethers the A-Tn5 transposase fusion protein. After its activation, transposase adds adapters for paired-end DNA sequencing, and high-resolution fragment libraries are generated \cite{kaya2019cut,kaya2020efficient}. In CUT\&RUN protocol, micrococcal nuclease (MNase) is tethered by the antibody and when activated cuts the DNA at the borders of TF binding \cite{skene2017efficient}. These novel methods allow for very low cell number input and profiling of TFs in single-cells (CUT\&Tag is better suited for this application) and promise a more accurate determination of the TF-bound genomic locations by providing high resolution and exceptionally low background \cite{skene2017efficient,kaya2019cut,kaya2020efficient}.

    Even with new technologies emerging every year, ChIP-seq remains an important method for TF binding profiling. A vast amount of ChIP-seq data is publicly available, which might not be fully explored. This motivates researchers to work on novel computational methods that would allow them to study this data even further.

    \subsection{Databases of TF-DNA models}

    With an increased amount of available data on TF-DNA interactions, the effort to start collecting DNA motifs was made. Today, several repositories store TF DNA binding motifs. For example, JASPAR, TRANSFAC, HOCOMOCO, and CIS-BP \cite{rauluseviciute2024jaspar,matys2006transfac,wingender2008transfac}. For the users of such resources, it is important to understand what data is stored and how it is retrieved and processed. JASPAR open-access database stores DNA binding motifs from six taxa that were manually curated. JASPAR motifs are discovered from experimental data, such as ChIP-seq or DAP-seq, or mined as PFMs from various resources (such as the selection of CIS-BP motifs) and publications. CORE collection motifs are supported by independent evidence found by curators. In addition to motif collections, JASPAR provides analyzed data, such as motif clusters, and a variety of tools \cite{rauluseviciute2024jaspar}. TRANSFAC also stores curated DNA motifs with experimentally determined and predicted binding sites. However, the full database is not open-access \cite{matys2006transfac,wingender2008transfac}. HOCOMOCO is a publicly available resource that compiles and stores human and mouse TF binding motifs produced using ChIP-seq or HT-SELEX data. The motifs in this database are human-curated and automatically benchmarked \cite{vorontsov2024hocomoco}. Finally, CIS-BP is also open-access and contains motifs for more than a thousand TFs from 131 diverse eukaryotes. Motifs are generated using \ac{PBM}, SELEX, or ChIP-seq data, but are also mined from other motif resources, such as TRANSFAC. A unique feature of CIS-BP is that the database offers inferred motifs. Based on the similarity of the DBDs, two TFs are binding similar motifs, therefore it is possible to infer a binding profile from one TF to a closely related other TF \cite{weirauch2014determination}.

    Even if current databases contain thousands of motifs from various species, it is important to keep these resources updated by adding new data and refining the old. Finally, new ways of TF-DNA modeling (such as deep-learning models) motivate the expansion or creation of new databases that could offer a variety of TF-DNA interaction models to the community.

    Prediction of TF-DNA interactions

    TF-DNA binding models can be used to predict putative TFBSs in the genome. There are various tools to perform motif scanning, for example, PWMScan \cite{weidemuller2021transcription}.
    
    However, it is known that genome scanning with a motif of interest results in numerous false positives, known as the futility theorem \cite{wasserman2004applied}. Therefore, it can be challenging to identify the functional TF binding sites.


    Current experimental techniques aim to identify TF binding, but in practice, the data reflect direct and indirect or non-specific TF binding \cite{furey2012chip}. It is challenging to separate the two signals computationally. In addition, while the TFs interact with the DNA through short motifs, the ChIP-seq peaks are usually hundreds of base pairs long. The ChIP-eat computational approach was developed to identify TFBSs that are directly bound by the TF of interest. The method combines ChIP-seq peak data and PWMs to produce sets of direct TFBSs ({Figure \ref{unibind}}) \cite{puig2021unibind,gheorghe2019map}. Given a set of peaks and an optimized PWM of profiled TF, the TFBS enrichment zone is detected. Automatic thresholding identifies the set of TFBSs that are closest to the peak summit and have the highest PWM scores, which is an indication of direct TF-DNA interactions \cite{puig2021unibind,gheorghe2019map}. The resulting TFBSs are of the size of the DNA binding motif. TFBSs from 9 species are provided to the community as a UniBind database \cite{puig2021unibind}.
