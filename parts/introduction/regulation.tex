\chapter{Transcription regulation}

Multicellular organisms, such as humans, are formed by thousands of cell types. However, the genome in every one of those cells is identical. If the recipe is the same, how is it possible to have so many cell types that manage to work together and run such a complex organism? The Human Genome project expected that once we have a complete sequence of the genome, it will be fairly straightforward to solve the causes of diseases, and easier to understand human evolution and development. But as it turned out, the majority of the human genome (98\%) does not code for proteins but is essential for the regulation of all expressed genes \cite{pang2023identification}. The genome is much more mysterious, and we are still studying its complexity \cite{kumar2016human}.

Processes that are not directly associated with the DNA sequence, are responsible for controlling the genome and its gene expression. Such mechanisms are termed epigenetic mechanisms (epi- means "over, outside") \cite{jablonka2002changing}. DNA methylation, histone tail modifications, and micro RNA are all contributing to the regulation of gene expression transcription regulation \cite{mitsis2020transcription}. Genomic DNA is constantly being approached by regulatory proteins and other small molecules. The major players in transcriptional regulation are \ac{TFs}. Without TFs RNA polymerase and the basic transcriptional machinery would not be able to approach genes and begin transcription. TFs act in different modes and can find and bind regulatory regions --- promoters and enhancers. Bound TFs attract Mediator proteins and Pol II and form a loop that brings enhancers and promoter regions close in space even though they can be separated by kilobases in a linear space ({Figure \ref{regions_loop}}). The activation of gene expression is tightly regulated and is cell type- and time-specific.

  \section{Cis-regulatory regions}

  Regulatory regions in the eukaryotic genome are segments of the DNA that are associated with the capability to increase or decrease transcription. These regions are termed \ac{CRRs}. They are crucial for gene expression regulation because they contain a set of short DNA sequences, \ac{TFBSs}, which are targeted by TFs \cite{wasserman2004applied}. CRRs include promoters, enhancers, and silencers.

    \subsection{Promoters}

    Promoters are located at the 5' end region of the gene ({Figure \ref{regions_loop}}A) and are crucial for gene expression regulation. Core promoters are located 40 bp upstream or downstream from the transcription start site (TSS) and are minimal regions needed for transcription initiation \cite{haberle2016promoter,kadonaga2012perspectives}. Promoters have specific DNA sequence elements important for gene regulation. For example, the TATA box element in the core promoter is bound by TBP, the Initiator sequence is one of the most common elements and encompasses the +1 TSS, while different sequence motifs can be bound by specific TFs \cite{villao2023plant,wasserman2004applied,haberle2016promoter,kadonaga2012perspectives}. Another characteristic common for human promoters is the abundance of CpG dinucleotides. CpGs are targets for cytosine methylation, which is one of the gene regulatory mechanisms \cite{wasserman2004applied,saxonov2006genome}. Around 70\% of human promoters near TSSs contain a \ac{CGI}, which is a region with a high frequency of CpGs \cite{deaton2011cpg,saxonov2006genome,haberle2016promoter}.

    Based on promoter composition, nucleosome positioning, and what genes they regulate, promoters can be classified into four main types: type I, II, III, and IV \cite{haberle2016promoter}. Type I promoters are adult tissue-specific and their transcription starts at the precise position. These promoters have a TATA box around 30 bp upstream from the TSS and nucleosomes are distributed in a disordered fashion along the sequence. Type II promoters control the expression of the housekeeping genes and transcription can be initiated at several locations. Type III promoters regulate developmental genes and also have a broad initiation pattern. Interestingly, these promoters depend on the input from distal enhancers. Type IV promoters are transcriptional machinery-specific and, like type I, have a sharp transcription initiation pattern. In type II, III, and IV promoters there is a nucleosome-free region with precisely positioned two nucleosomes downstream and upstream. In addition, all three types of promoters have CGIs covering the TSSs \cite{haberle2016promoter}.

    \subsection{Enhancers and silencers}

    Enhancers are distal regulatory regions that activate transcription by acting on a promoter over large genomic distances ({Figure \ref{regions_loop}}) \cite{smith2014enhancer}. The distance from the enhancer to the promoter it acts on can vary from several kilobases to megabases \cite{field2020evaluating,karpinska2023role}. The size of enhancers also varies from less than 100 bp long elements to 10--100 kb sequences, which are sometimes referred to as super-enhancers \cite{field2020evaluating,hnisz2013super,smith2014enhancer}. They often act in a spatially and temporally restricted manner, which means that enhancers are cell-type specific and are active only at specific time points \cite{field2020evaluating}. Enhancers are approached by transcription factors and bind specific DNA patterns. Multiple TFs can bind one enhancer, depending on how many potential TFBSs that enhancer has \cite{wu2019super,field2020evaluating}. Fairly recently it has been discovered that enhancer DNA is being transcribed into non-coding RNA, termed \ac{eRNA} ({Figure \ref{regions_loop}}B) \cite{smith2014enhancer}. These RNAs promote the functions of enhancers by recruiting TFs, promoting enhancer-promoter looping, and influencing chromatin conformation or histone acetylation status \cite{wu2019super,field2020evaluating}.

    Enhancer-promoter looping occurs in isolated chromatin structures - loops and \ac{TADs} ({Figure \ref{loops_tads}}). Cohesin is a ring-like structure that holds the chromatin loops together letting enhancer and promoter communicate through complexes of proteins \cite{smith2014enhancer,field2020evaluating,davidson2021genome}. The formation of loops is known as loop extrusion. Another loop-extruding factor is protein CTCF. It binds DNA at the TAD boundaries, this way informing where to stop the loop extrusion process closing the entire domain ({Figure \ref{loops_tads}}A) \cite{davidson2021genome}. Multiple loops held or not by cohesin can be found in one TAD \cite{karpinska2023role,luppino2020cohesin,davidson2021genome} ({Figure \ref{loops_tads}}B-C). CTCF binding motif orientation is important for the loop extrusion since two CTCF proteins bound convergently are required to close the loop \cite{davidson2021genome}. It is described as insulating a particular region \cite{field2020evaluating}. The influence of the enhancers is most of the time, but not always, limited to the genes that are within the same loop \cite{karpinska2023role,luppino2020cohesin,symmons2014functional}. One enhancer can regulate multiple genes, while one gene can be regulated by multiple enhancers.

    Opposite to the enhancers, silencers are DNA sequences that are bound by the repressor proteins to silence gene transcription \cite{spitz2012transcription}. Even though most of the research is focused on enhancer and promoter identification, silencers are interesting and important regions to research. It is known that silencers can also be tissue-specific and that they operate within TADs through short- or long-range interactions \cite{pang2020systematic,pang2023identification}. These regions are marked by specific chromatin marks, such as \ac{H4K20me} \cite{pang2020systematic,beck2012pr}. Silencers are targeted by repressive TFs and through looping communicate with the promoter of the gene, whose expression needs to be repressed \cite{pang2023identification}.

  \section{Transcription factors}

  Transcription factors (TFs) are key regulators of transcription \cite{mitsis2020transcription,weidemuller2021transcription}. About 10\% of the human protein-coding genome codes for transcription factors \cite{levine2003transcription}. TFs act as a bridge between the cell signaling pathways and gene expression. Through receptors, external cues trigger post-translational modification cascades that eventually result in the modulation of a set of TFs, which are then recruited at the gene regulatory regions \cite{weidemuller2021transcription}. Which TFs are modified depends on the stimuli, cell type, and state. In this section TFs that directly interpret the genome by binding to the specific DNA sequence patterns will be discussed. Short DNA sequences recognized by TFs are known as DNA motifs. The function of TFs is often studied together with the DNA motifs that they recognize \cite{lambert2018human}. There are various experimental methods to identify TF-DNA interactions and a variety of computational tools to model and represent those interactions. Most of the experimental techniques are limited to investigating individual TFs. However, in reality, TFs often cooperate, compete for the same binding site, or interact with the DNA indirectly \cite{mitsis2020transcription}. All these are complex TF activity mechanisms that are still difficult to investigate and interpret with the currently available tools.

    \subsection{Protein structures and classification}

    Eukaryotic transcription factor proteins have at least two domains, one for DNA binding and one for activation or repression. Optionally there are \ac{PPI} or effector domains to regulate TFs activity \cite{zhu2011mapping,minchin201393,wingender2013criteria}. \ac{DBD} recognize and bind DNA motifs. Thousands of protein-DNA structures are already solved and available in resources, such as \ac{PDB} \cite{yesudhas2017proteins,berman2000protein,wingender2013criteria}. For example, the \ac{bHLH} domain proteins have two \( \alpha \)-helices connected by a turn. These domains are known to dimerize, and each monomer recognizes one side of a symmetric DNA sequence ({Figure \ref{dbd_classification}}A). bHLH proteins bind at the major groove of the DNA molecule \cite{yesudhas2017proteins}. Homeodomain-type DBDs are highly conserved. POU5F1, also known as OCT4, has two DBDs, which are binding cooperatively at the major groove ({Figure \ref{dbd_classification}}B).

    DBDs are extremely well-conserved and used to classify TFs into families and classes, where proteins with a very similar amino acid sequence in the DBD are grouped. It is known that TFs from the same class or family tend to recognize the same or highly similar DNA patterns ({Figure \ref{dbd_classification}}C) \cite{mitsis2020transcription}. This is an important feature that could help to define binding specificities of understudied TFs \cite{wingender2013criteria}. In addition, DBD can provide information about the protein function. For example, homeodomain TFs are often involved in developmental processes \cite{vaquerizas2009census}. In 2009 23 human TF families were defined, where 80\% of all known human TFs at the time had C\textsubscript{2}H\textsubscript{2} zinc-finger, homeodomain, or HLH DBDs \cite{vaquerizas2009census}. Later the classification was updated several times, also expanding from humans to all mammalian TFs. One of the most extensive classifications of mammalian transcription factors, TFClass, includes superclass, class, family, subfamily, and genus categories \cite{wingender2018tfclass, wingender2013criteria,wingender2015tfclass}. TF families and class categories are the most used to annotate TFs, for example, in the JASPAR database. The most up-to-date version classifies more than 39,000 TFs from up to 41 mammalian species. TF DBDs were annotated and TFs were classified using phylogenetic analyses \cite{wingender2018tfclass}. More recently, a similar classification of plant TFs was released. Many DBDs present in plants, but not found in mammals were hindering the full classification of the plant TFs. Plant-TFClass addressed that by adding 8 new classes and 37 new families to classify most of the 56 plant TF types \cite{blanc2024plant}. This recent development in TF classification highlights the need to consistently revise and update current classifications, considering other taxa, such as nematodes or insects.

  \section{Transcription factor binding}

  Transcription factors bind cis-regulatory regions, promoters, and enhancers, to regulate associated gene transcription. Depending on the sequence context (and available co-factors), TF can either activate or repress \cite{lambert2018human}. It is known that interactions with CRRs are highly cell-type-specific. The location where a TF resides in DNA is referred to as a transcription factor binding site (TFBS). A promoter or an enhancer can have multiple TFBSs of the same or different TF. Transcription factors generally have a binding preference for particular DNA patterns --- motifs \cite{boeva2016analysis}.

  How do transcription factors find these small binding sites located in a long DNA molecule and surrounded by a variety of proteins and molecules in the nucleus? The four main forms of TF motion in the environment with DNA are (1) 3D Brownian diffusion in the nucleoplasm, (2) 1D sliding along the DNA, where TF binds DNA in a non-specific fashion, (3) already bound TF binds a nearby region (intersegmental transfer), (4) TF unbinds from a region, briefly diffuses, and rebinds to a nearby section of the same region (hopping) \cite{schmidt2014integrated}. Each of these motions takes a different time for a TF to find a target region.

  The way a transcription factor interacts with the DNA influences transcription regulatory mechanisms. TF binding varies from direct binding to the DNA (most well-studied) to indirect and cooperative binding \cite{boeva2016analysis,inukai2017transcription}. TF binding to the DNA depends on various features that co-exist together. For example, DNA modifications, such as DNA methylation, can dictate whether a TF will bind. DNA shape, presence of co-factors, or sequence variants can also determine TF binding \cite{inukai2017transcription}. It is important to understand these mechanisms. Through the different binding modes, the complexity of gene transcription regulation can be increased. It is essential to ensure cell-type-specific transcription.
  
  In this section, direct, indirect, and cooperative TF binding will be overviewed. In addition, pioneer TFs and DNA methylation impact will be briefly presented. Finally, these TF binding mechanisms were deciphered thanks to the various experimental techniques developed over the years. The most popular and most used methodologies will be described.

    \subsection{Direct and indirect TF binding}

    Direct and indirect TF binding is an often discussed topic. 
    For example, transcription factor Spi-1/PU.1 can bind to DNA directly and indirectly. Direct binding of Spi-1/PU.1 to the consensus motif $ AGGAA $ leads to transcriptional activation, while indirect binding through a partner GATA1 to the $ GATA $ motif may lead to transcriptional repression \cite{boeva2016analysis}.


    Computational approaches to distinguish direct from indirect TF-DNA interactions are discussed in the section about computational modeling.

    \subsection{Cooperative transcription factor binding}

    Cooperativity (or co-binding) of TFs on regulatory regions, such as enhancers, is an essential transcription regulation mechanism \cite{long2016ever}. Interaction with other sequence-specific or non-DNA binding co-factors can alter binding specificity \cite{inukai2017transcription}. Cooperative binding does not always mean that two TFs will bind the DNA directly, they can be cooperating through protein-protein interactions or binding DNA indirectly \cite{mitsis2020transcription}.

    The cooperativity between the TFs that have DNA binding specificity can be analyzed through their binding DNA patterns. The rules to describe the cooperativity between the TFs are often referred to as motif grammar \cite{long2016ever}. It is the presence of certain motifs, their order, the spacing between them, and also the orientation of those motifs \cite{long2016ever}. The two motifs can also overlap with each other, that way creating a composite motif that would be co-bound by a dimeric TF. Such cases are often very difficult to investigate due to the limitations of experimental and computational tools. Research evidence shows that some TFs require a very strict motif grammar for their cooperativity, while others bind with a variety of spacings, which is described as a relaxed grammar or soft syntax \cite{avsec2021base}. The orientation of the DNA motifs alone can be a strong determinant of transcription activity. For example, Spi-1/PU.1 is known to modulate gene expression in erythroid cells and promote oncogenesis. It was also shown to cooperate with KLF family TFs. When the Klf-1 motif is in direct orientation in the promoter regions, Spi-1/PU.1 activates transcription, while the genes are repressed when Klf-1 binds the motif in reverse orientation \cite{boeva2016analysis}.

    Currently, three main models describe cooperative TF binding at the enhancer regions: enhanceosome, billboard, and TF collective model ({Figure \ref{tf_cooperativity_models}}) \cite{spitz2012transcription,long2016ever}. The two main models are enhanceosome, which requires a rigid motif grammar, and the billboard model, which allows soft motif syntax and the presence of specific TFs is more important than the order, orientation, and spacing of the motifs ({Figure \ref{tf_cooperativity_models}}A-B). Regulatory regions that are bound by TFs using an enhanceosome model are highly evolutionary conserved and TFs are binding directly to the strictly organized motifs. ({Figure \ref{tf_cooperativity_models}}A). Regions following the billboard model oppositely are less conserved and cooperativity is indirect, but TFs also bind directly to the DNA motifs ({Figure \ref{tf_cooperativity_models}}B). The third model, TF collective, falls somewhere in between the first two ({Figure \ref{tf_cooperativity_models}}C). In this case, the regions are also less conserved. TFs can bind both directly and indirectly, motifs are not always present and can be organized flexibly. Finally, a mixture of all models can be included in one enhancer. Such regions have a variable sequence conservation, TFs can bind both directly and indirectly, some motifs have a strict grammar, some are distributed more flexibly or motifs are not present at all \cite{long2016ever}.

    It is essential to better understand how different transcription factors work together to regulate transcription. The cooperativity of TFs provides an incredible complexity of regulatory mechanisms, which allows spatial and temporal control of cell differentiation and growth. Specific experimental techniques, such as CAP-SELEX, have been developed to profile dimeric TF binding (see section below), but they are still limited. More opportunities to look into TF cooperativity are provided by computational tools. Simple computational models, such as position weight matrices, are not well suited to represent cooperative binding, but deep learning techniques nowadays have a lot of potential in this area (read more about computational models in the further section).

    \subsection{Pioneering}

    A specific group of transcription factors can interact with the nucleosome-protected closed chromatin and trigger its opening for other TFs and proteins \cite{barral2023pioneer,long2016ever}. These TFs are known as pioneer transcription factors or master regulators \cite{long2016ever}. FOXA and GATA4 were the first factors detected to engage the enhancers in development and their occupancy preceded occupancy of the other TFs that bind to those enhancers \cite{zaret2011pioneer,cirillo2002opening,yu2020pioneer}. Today, well-characterized pioneer TFs include SOX2, KLF4, and OCT4, which are also known as Yamanaka factors because they can be used to induce pluripotency \cite{yu2020pioneer}. It was shown that pioneers bind the condensed chromatin before the activation of transcription, therefore showing their essentiality for region transcriptional activation \cite{yu2020pioneer}. It is known that in some cases (p53) (SOX2, KLF4, and OCT4) it is enough for a pioneer TF to recognize a partial DNA motif to bind closed chromatin, in other scenarios pioneers prefer binding near the nucleosome edge and their binding affinity is stronger than to the sites located in the naked DNA \cite{yu2020pioneer}. After binding, pioneer TFs open closed chromatin locally first. Then ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers, such as the SWI/SNF remodeling complex, are recruited to promote nucleosome eviction in the region \cite{barral2023pioneer}. Finally, acetyltransferases are recruited to modify histone tails and activate the cis-regulatory element \cite{barral2023pioneer,boeva2016analysis}.

    \subsection{Impact of methylation to the DNA binding}

    DNA modifications impact how transcription factors bind \cite{wang2019identification}. The most extensively studied epigenetic DNA modification is DNA methylation. Cytosines in the DNA molecule are modified by adding a methyl group in the context of CpG dinucleotides, generating \ac{5mC} ({Figure \ref{tf_methylation_binding}}A). This reaction is performed by "writers" --- \ac{DNMTs}, and the removal of methyl groups is conducted by \ac{TET} enzymes \cite{wang2019identification,schubeler2015function}. Generally, hypermethylation of CpGs in the promoter regions is associated with transcription repression, while gene body hypermethylation indicates active genes \cite{schubeler2015function,ball2009targeted}. Therefore, DNA methylation is very important for TFs that bind methylated motifs, and it has been shown that some prefer to interact with methylated sites or, in contrast, are prevented from binding to methylated motifs \cite{yin2017impact,schubeler2015function}. For example, the HOXB13 protein has three key residues that contribute to the preferential mCpG binding ({Figure \ref{tf_methylation_binding}}B-C) \cite{yin2017impact}. HOXB13 can also bind to the unmethylated motif, which then does not contain a CpG dinucleotide ({Figure \ref{tf_methylation_binding}}C). A portion of TFs (for example, MAX) are not influenced by methylation even though their motifs contain a CpG \cite{yin2017impact}. Changes in the methylation patterns can lead to aberrant TF binding, affecting transcription regulation and possibly causing diseases.

    However, the way TF-DNA interactions are most commonly modeled today does not take into account methylated CpGs and position interdependency, which is of special relevance when analyzing CpG dinucleotides (read more about computational modeling of TF-DNA interactions in the next section). The presence of a CpG in the binding motif does not inform whether it needs to be methylated for the TF to bind. There are special attempts to model TF binding to the methylated DNA. For example, the MEDEMO toolkit analyzes TF binding motifs by including information about DNA methylation \cite{grau2023widespread}. The simplest way to include methylation information is to extend the alphabet where 5mC is replaced with M and G on the opposite strand with an H. Such methylation-aware genome can then be used to train methylation-aware TF binding models. These can therefore inform about methylation sensitivity ({Figure \ref{tf_methylation_binding}}D) and allow the identification of differential TF binding regions in ChIP-seq data \cite{grau2023widespread}.

    \subsection{Experimental methods to detect TF-DNA interactions}

    Over the years, multiple experimental methods were developed to detect the binding preferences of the transcription factors. Broadly these methods can be categorized into \textit{in vitro} and \textit{in vitro}. The majority of the methods are limited to investigating the interactions of a single TF at the time. The most popular and relevant methods in these categories will be briefly described in this section.

      \subsubsection{\textit{In vitro}}

      \textit{In vitro} methods mainly aim to identify TF binding sequence preferences. For example, \ac{SELEX} or PBM. Other methods, such as EMSA, can inform about the binding affinity \cite{geertz2010experimental}. The biggest limitation of \textit{in vitro} methods is that by design they are not able to give information about the biological context of the TF-DNA interaction.

      SELEX is a qualitative method developed decades ago, but high-throughput (HT-SELEX) and other versions are extensively used today \cite{kinzler1990gene,jolma2010multiplexed}. The basic principle of SELEX is the incubation of a purified TF with a pool of random DNA oligonucleotides ({Figure \ref{chip_selex}}A). Random sequences bound to the protein are collected, amplified, and incubated with the TF again. After multiple cycles of incubation, separation, elution, and amplification, the sequences that have the highest affinity are selected and sent to the sequencing procedure. The resulting sequencing data is analyzed to determine the TF binding sequence preference \cite{geertz2010experimental,jolma2010multiplexed}. Differently from a regular SELEX, in HT-SELEX one can study multiple transcription factors simultaneously. However, since the oligonucleotides are random, in SELEX it is not guaranteed that the sequences bound with the highest affinity reflect the functional binding preferences in a living cell.

      One variation of the SELEX protocol is of particular interest because it allows studying dimer TF sequence preferences. In the \ac{CAP-SELEX} protocol oligonucleotides are incubated with two TFs that are flagged with different flags. During two consecutive affinity purification steps, only those sequences bound by both TFs are selected for amplification \cite{jolma2015dna}. This technique gives an insight into how specifically two TFs can bind the same sequence. For example, we can determine preferred spacings or overlaps between two individual motifs and motif orientation \cite{jolma2015dna}.

      A high-throughput technique that could be classified in between \textit{in vitro} and \textit{in vivo} is called \ac{DAP-seq}. In DAP-seq, the libraries of sequences are constructed using native DNA, preserving cell-specific modifications. This provides increased specificity compared to methods, such as SELEX. The library of sequences is then incubated with \textit{in vitro}-expressed and tagged TF. TF-bound sequences are purified and sequenced. Finally, sequences are then mapped to the genome of origin to identify locations preferentially bound by a TF \cite{bartlett2017mapping}.

      \subsubsection{\textit{In vivo}}

      Arguably the most popular technique to map TF-DNA interactions in living cells is \ac{ChIP-seq} \cite{johnson2007genome}. The ChIP-seq process starts with cross-linking (using formaldehyde) of the DNA and proteins in the living cells ({Figure \ref{chip_selex}}B). Then DNA is randomly fragmented using sonication leaving DNA segments bound and unbound by proteins. In a crucial step, a specific antibody is used to bind the TF of interest. TF-DNA complexes bound by an antibody are selected by washing away everything else. Protein-DNA cross-links are reversed and DNA fragments, previously protected by the TF, are purified. These DNA sequences are then subjected to sequencing. After sequencing the data is analyzed by mapping the sequencing reads to a genome and identifying those genomic regions that are enriched by TF binding \cite{johnson2007genome,park2009chip}.

      The biggest advantage of \textit{in vivo} techniques, such as ChIP-seq, is that the TF binding locations are determined in a specific tissue or cell line, thus reflecting the TF binding preferences in physiological conditions \cite{park2009chip}. However, this comes with a few limitations. TF binding and regulatory mechanisms are complex, so \textit{in vivo} binding is influenced by several factors that affect the outcome of the experiment. Differences in TF-DNA affinity, protein-protein interactions, indirect binding, and sequence biases can all affect how results are interpreted \cite{jolma2010multiplexed,rhee2011comprehensive,rossi2018simplified}. Other limitations of the ChIP-seq technology include studying only one TF at a time and that the immunoprecipitation requires a high-quality antibody, which can be difficult to acquire if the protein of interest is relatively unknown or poorly annotated \cite{park2009chip}.

      In ChIP-seq, the DNA fragment sizes are variable and are longer (hundreds of base pairs) than the actual TF-protected region, because DNA is sheared randomly \cite{welch2017data,rhee2011comprehensive}. Therefore, the TF binding sites are not inferred in base pair resolution. Computational analysis and motif search are insufficient to identify all binding locations in ChIP-seq derived regions \cite{rhee2011comprehensive,yeh2022chip}. To overcome this limitation ChIP-exo technique was developed. The protocol has an additional step of immunoprecipitate treatment by 5` to 3` lambda exonuclease, which digests the unprotected ends of the DNA fragment bound by a TF. This allows us to get precise, nearly single-base resolution, genomic locations bound by a TF \cite{rhee2011comprehensive,yeh2022chip}. The length of such regions is 36-100 bp \cite{welch2017data}. A simplified version of the ChIP-exo protocol, ChIP-nexus, was developed in 2015 \cite{rossi2018simplified,welch2017data}.

      The latest methods for TF binding region identification \textit{in vivo} include CUT\&Tag and CUT\&RUN \cite{kaya2019cut}. Both techniques are similar and are based on specific antibodies recognizing the TF of interest. In CUT\&Tag, the antibody tethers the A-Tn5 transposase fusion protein. After its activation, transposase adds adapters for paired-end DNA sequencing, and high-resolution fragment libraries are generated \cite{kaya2019cut,kaya2020efficient}. In CUT\&RUN protocol, micrococcal nuclease (MNase) is tethered by the antibody and when activated cuts the DNA at the borders of TF binding \cite{skene2017efficient}. These novel methods allow for very low cell number input and profiling of TFs in single-cells (CUT\&Tag is better suited for this application) and promise a more accurate determination of the TF-bound genomic locations by providing high resolution and exceptionally low background \cite{skene2017efficient,kaya2019cut,kaya2020efficient}.

      Even with new technologies emerging every year, ChIP-seq remains an important method for TF binding profiling. A vast amount of ChIP-seq data is publicly available, which might not be fully explored. This motivates researchers to work on novel computational methods that would allow them to study this data even further.

  \section{Computational modeling of TF-DNA interactions}

  Various experimental approaches for TF-DNA interaction analysis result in a set of sequences or genomic regions that indicate TF specificity towards DNA patterns. This allows us to computationally model TF-DNA interactions. A collection of models is available today, spanning from simple and most widely used \ac{PWMs} to the most advanced deep learning models. This section will overview different models, and techniques that generate them and will present a few resources that store such models.

    \subsection{Transcription factor DNA binding models}

      \subsubsection{Position weight matrices (PWMs)}

      To interpret TF binding to the regulatory regions, we need ways to represent the DNA binding. The most popular and widely used model is the \ac{PWMs} (also called weight matrices or \ac{PSSMs}) ({Figure \ref{tf_dna_models}}). PWM is a matrix of numbers of each possible base (A, C, G, or T for a DNA model) at each position in a TF binding site \cite{stormo2013modeling,wasserman2004applied}. It provides a quantitative description of the known \ac{TFBSs} \cite{wasserman2004applied}. From a set of aligned sequences (for example, processed experimental data or sequences collected from literature) that are bound by a TF of interest, a \ac{PFM} can be computed ({Figure \ref{tf_dna_models}}A-B). A PFM, or a count matrix, is created by counting the occurrences of each nucleotide at each position. Counts can be converted into probabilities to build a \ac{PPM} ({Figure \ref{tf_dna_models}}C) using an equation

      \begin{equation}
        p(b,i)=\frac{f_{b,i}+s(b)}{N+\sum\limits_{b'\in\{A,C,G,T\}}s(b')}
        \label{ppm}
      \end{equation}
      where $ f_{b,i} $ is counts of base $ b $ in position $ i $; $ N $ is the number of sites; $ p(b,i) $ is a probability of base $ b $ in position $ i $; $ s(b) $ is a pseudocount function. Pseudocounts can be added to each cell of the PFM to eliminate zeroes when the number of binding sites is very small \cite{wasserman2004applied}.

      A PWM ({Figure \ref{tf_dna_models}}D) can then be constructed by dividing the nucleotide probabilities by expected background probabilities and converting the resulting values to a log scale. What background probabilities are chosen can be debatable, but the simplest model assumed equal 0.25 background probabilities for each base. The computation can be done using an equation

      \begin{equation}
        W_{b,i}=log_{2}\frac{p(b,i)}{p(b)}
        \label{pwm}
      \end{equation}
      where $ p(b) $ is a background probability of base $ b $; $ p(b,i) $ is a probability of base $ b $ in position $ i $; $ W_{b,i} $ is a PWM value or base $ b $ in position $ i $.

      Frequency or count matrices can be represented in multiple formats. For example, JASPAR format ({Figure \ref{tf_dna_models}}B) visualizes the counts, while MEME format visualizes the probabilities of each nucleotide in each position ({Figure \ref{tf_dna_models}}C). Some computational tools require the matrices to be formatted in a specific way, so it is useful to be familiar with different count and probability representations.

      It is difficult to interpret the actual DNA pattern that is preferentially bound by a TF only by looking at the PWM or PFM matrices. Therefore, TF-DNA interaction specificity is usually visualized as a sequence logo ({Figure \ref{tf_dna_models}}E). The logo gives information about the frequency of each base at each position but also provides information on the degree of conservation of each motif position. Sequence logo visualizes \ac{IC} in bits, which is a basic unit of information and spans from 0 to 2. IC can be computed from probabilities using an equation

      \begin{equation}
        D_{i}=2+\sum\limits_{b}p_{b,i}log_{2}p_{b,i}
        \label{ic}
      \end{equation}
      where $ D_{i} $ is the IC in position $ i $, $ p(b,i) $ is a probability of base $ b $ in position $ i $.

      All computational models used to model TF-DNA interactions are based on certain assumptions. One of the biggest limitations of PWMs is that these models assume positions in the binding sequence to be independent \cite{wasserman2004applied,mathelier2013next}. In reality, what nucleotide is located at an adjoining position is important for transcription factor binding and was demonstrated through biochemical, statistical, and crystal structure analysis \cite{mathelier2013next}. For example, CpG dinucleotide presence is discussed in the section above. Another possible caveat of the PWMs is that they are limited in modeling compositional binding since homodimers and heterodimers can have a variable spacing between the two binding patterns. PWMs are then unable to predict TFBSs appropriately \cite{wasserman2004applied,mathelier2013next}. PWM models can be expanded to capture additional information, such as DNA methylation. Other TF-DNA interaction models include \ac{TFFMs}, DNA shape-based models. However, even with their limitations, the availability and ease of use keep PFMs and PWMs the primary method to model TF-DNA interactions.

      \subsubsection{Other models}

      TFFM models were introduced in 2013 and are based on \ac{HMMs} and model both position interdependence within TFBSs and variable length motifs, which simple PWM models cannot achieve \cite{mathelier2013next}. Through protein binding array studies it was demonstrated that dependencies are stronger between the neighboring positions in the binding site compared to others \cite{zhao2012improved}. The HMMs allow the modeling of flexible motif lengths because the unconstrained potential for gaps is incorporated at any position, while interdependencies are addressed via a variable-order Bayesian network to model TFBS positions \cite{mathelier2013next}. To visualize TFFMs and interpret position interdependencies a new graphical representation, the TFFM logo, was created. It demonstrates the dinucleotide dependencies but is not able to visualize the variable length or spacing of the modeled motif \cite{mathelier2013next}.

      Another TF-DNA interaction property that is not addressed by either PWMs or TFFMs is TF binding specificity towards DNA shape. PWMs (base readout) can be combined with DNA shape features (shape readout), such as helix twist, minor groove width, propeller twist, and roll \cite{mathelier2016dna}. These properties are computed by the DNAshape method \cite{zhou2013dnashape}. Putative TFBSs are predicted in the ChIP-seq peaks using PWMs (or TFFMs). DNA shape features are then predicted at each position of the TFBS. With this information the classifier is trained to discriminate TF-bound regions from the background \cite{mathelier2016dna}.

      \subsubsection{\textit{De novo} motif discovery}

      When analyzing TF-DNA interaction data, such as ChIP-seq, one can find over-represented sequence motifs using existing collections of DNA binding motifs (see the section below about the use of TF-DNA binding models). However, motif collections are always limited, so to discover new motifs, \textit{de novo} motif discovery approaches have been developed \cite{boeva2016analysis}. \textit{De novo} motif discovery is a process during which both PWM and the sites are inferred simultaneously \cite{stormo2013modeling}. There are several computational tools developed to discover over-represented patterns in a set of DNA sequences. For example, MEME, STREME, RSAT \textit{peak-motifs}, and HOMER. Motifs can be discovered in sequences derived from experimental procedures, such as SELEX, \ac{PBM}, and ChIP-based techniques \cite{boeva2016analysis}.

      MEME is a classical computational tool for the detection of over-represented motifs \cite{bailey1994fitting}. It was developed to analyze short and small numbers of DNA sequences, therefore, this limits its usage for the analysis of ChIP-seq peaks. To address this, the authors of MEME most recently developed STREME, which allows efficient DNA or RNA analysis of large datasets \cite{bailey2021streme}. STREME finds the motifs that vary from 3 to 30 bp in length and evaluates the enrichment of found matches in the primary set compared to the background \cite{bailey2021streme}.

      RSAT \textit{peak-motifs} is a computational pipeline that integrates several motif discovery algorithms, namely oligo-, position-, and dyad-analysis. Each of them can be used as independent tools. All algorithms are generally searching for exceptional motifs in a set of sequences \cite{thomas2011rsat,thomas2012rsat,santana2022rsat}. Oligo-analysis detects over-represented words, while position-analysis identifies oligonucleotides with heterogeneous positional distributions in a provided set of sequences \cite{van1998extracting,helden2000statistical,thomas2011rsat}. By default, 6 and 7 bp length motifs are searched in word-based analysis. Expected word frequencies are estimated with a Markov model in the oligo-analysis \cite{thomas2012rsat}. Dyad-analysis focuses on the detection of spaced motifs, which can be bound by dimeric TFs \cite{helden2000discovering,thomas2011rsat}. In the yeast genome, it was observed that regulatory sites often contain pairs of highly conserved trinucleotides, separated by non-conserved positions \cite{helden2000discovering}. RSAT \textit{peak-motifs} can also perform differential analysis (oligo-diff) when the control set is provided \cite{thomas2012rsat}.

      HOMER was designed to analyze promoters and ChIP-seq data and searches for enriched motifs in one set of sequences compared to the background set. By default, HOMER motif discovery will search for motifs of lengths 8, 10, and 12 bp by exhaustively screening possible oligos in the target sets and comparing them to the background computes the enrichment using cumulative hypergeometric distribution. The top 200 oligos are selected to then compute PWM. With the custom background set, this differential motif discovery algorithm can be used to compare ChIP-seq peaks in one cell type with peaks in another cell type \cite{heinz2010simple}.

      All the above-mentioned computational tools have their strengths and weaknesses. HOMER and RSAT integrate a useful additional feature, where discovered motifs can be compared against known motif libraries (read more about motif comparison in the section below). However, one of the main limitations is that these tools aim to discover a single over-represented pattern in a set of sequences. To into the relationship between different patterns and possible combinatorics of TF-DNA interactions, specialized approaches are needed that would allow summarizing and interpretation of multiple discovered DNA motifs.

      \subsubsection{Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) for motif discovery}

      A different approach from the above, \ac{NMF}, could be used for DNA pattern discovery in sequences. NMF is one of the matrix factorization techniques. In general, all matrix factorization algorithms learn two matrices, amplitude, and pattern \cite{stein2018enter}.

      To apply NMF on genomic sequences, the nucleotide information must be one-hot encoded. It is a classical categorical data processing step for machine learning approaches. Each nucleotide is assigned a unique four-bit long code ({Figure \ref{nmf_motif_discovery}}A). Individual sequences are converted into a numerical array, which then can be passed to the NMF algorithm ({Figure \ref{nmf_motif_discovery}}B). In the learned amplitude matrix, each column stores continuous weights that describe the relative contribution of each sequence in each inferred component. On the other hand, each row in the pattern matrix describes the contribution of each position to a component. \cite{stein2018enter}. Therefore, these two matrices can be used to distinguish subsets of sequences with particular patterns that can then be represented as DNA sequence motifs. NMF has a hyperparameter $ k $, instructing how many components should be learned. For example, with $ k = 4 $ four DNA patterns are learned from an input set of DNA sequences ({Figure \ref{nmf_motif_discovery}}C).

      NMF is the basis of a computational tool, seqArchR, to identify position-specific DNA patterns in promoter regions and decipher promoter architectures \cite{nikumbh2023identifying}. It is known that certain DNA motifs inform the selection of TSSs and are located at fixed distances from them. The seqArchR package can identify TSS-directing motifs, such as TATA or nucleosome position signal, but also lineage-specific motifs. Defining combinations of these motifs can be used to classify promoter architectures and associate them with specific gene regulatory programs \cite{nikumbh2023identifying}.

      NMF algorithms take arrays as the input, which means that the input genomic sequences must be of the same length. Another important limitation is that sequence patterns that can be distinguished by NMF have to occur at fixed distances. Various patterns can be discovered in separate components, but there must be enough sequences with the same pattern at the same location. Lastly, a hyperparameter $ k $ can be difficult to define. There are various methodologies, such as the estimation of the cophenetic correlation coefficient, to define the number of components \cite{brunet2004metagenes}. However, there is no single best way to decide on this parameter.

    \subsection{Deep learning modeling approaches}

    Nowadays, it is machine learning, in particular deep learning, that is the most state-of-the-art approach for TF-DNA interaction modeling. These approaches promise to address a few important limitations of the simpler TF-DNA binding models, such as PWM. Some TFs do not have strong motif patterns that could be represented easily with the PWM \cite{wang2022towards}. Furthermore, the more complex TF-DNA interactions, such as TF cooperativity, are not obvious to represent with simple models. Finally, deep learning techniques can integrate multiple factors that define TF binding to the DNA. In addition to the DNA sequence context, chromatin accessibility or DNA methylation can be used to model TF binding \cite{wang2022towards}.

    The current methods can be roughly grouped into three groups: 1) sequence-based methods, which only require DNA sequence data as input and can distinguish regions that are bound by a specific TF or unbound. 2) sequence and shape-based methods, which in addition to the sequence data integrate DNA shape feature information. Such methods model the interplay between nucleotide-amino acid contacts and DNA structural features. 3) sequence and epigenomic profile-based methods, which together with DNA sequence data integrate epigenomic profiling data, such as chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, or histone modifications. Such models can predict cell-type-specific TF-DNA interactions that are not driven by the DNA sequence alone, but also by epigenetic mechanisms. Trained models are usually evaluated, and their performance is tested \cite{wang2022towards} ({Figure \ref{deep_learning_modeling}}). Motif discovery in the deep learning approaches is performed employing one of two main strategies: feature importance score (perturbation- or backpropagation-based methods) and filter-based approaches \cite{wang2022towards}.

    As mentioned previously, simple TF-DNA interaction models are not generally suited to represent TF cooperativity. Therefore, deep learning approaches are particularly relevant when it comes to deciphering and modeling cooperative transcription factor binding. One of the latest approaches that was used to predict binding profiles of TFs and demonstrated cooperative action is BPNet \cite{avsec2021base}. BPNet is a \ac{CNN} and models the interplay between regulatory sequence and TF binding profiles at base resolution. It consists of multiple convolutional layers and was developed to predict the ChIP-nexus signal in \ac{mESCs} for four TFs (Oct4, Sox2, Nanog, and Klf4). To interpret the results from the BPNet and retrieve the DNA motifs, another tool, TF-MoDISco, is required \cite{shrikumar2018technical}. Motifs are discovered by scanning the regions with high contribution scores, and then performing pairwise alignments and clustering. A motif is derived by averaging the contribution scores at each position for each nucleotide across the aligned sequences. The authors of BPNet in their study demonstrated that their analyzed TFs are binding using soft syntax rules, which means that there are no strict requirements on the numbers of nucleotides in between the two TFs co-binding the region \cite{avsec2021base}.

    \subsection{Databases of TF-DNA interaction models}

    With an increased amount of available data on TF-DNA interactions, the effort to start collecting DNA motifs was made. Today, several repositories store TF DNA binding motifs. For example, JASPAR, TRANSFAC, HOCOMOCO, and CIS-BP \cite{rauluseviciute2024jaspar,matys2006transfac,wingender2008transfac}. For the users of such resources, it is important to understand what data is stored and how it is retrieved and processed. JASPAR open-access database stores DNA binding motifs from six taxa that were manually curated. JASPAR motifs are discovered from experimental data, such as ChIP-seq or DAP-seq, or mined as PFMs from various resources (such as the selection of CIS-BP motifs) and publications. CORE collection motifs are supported by independent evidence found by curators. In addition to motif collections, JASPAR provides analyzed data, such as motif clusters, and a variety of tools \cite{rauluseviciute2024jaspar}. TRANSFAC also stores curated DNA motifs with experimentally determined and predicted binding sites. However, the full database is not open-access \cite{matys2006transfac,wingender2008transfac}. HOCOMOCO is a publicly available resource that compiles and stores human and mouse TF binding motifs produced using ChIP-seq or HT-SELEX data. The motifs in this database are human-curated and automatically benchmarked \cite{vorontsov2024hocomoco}. Finally, CIS-BP is also open-access and contains motifs for more than a thousand TFs from 131 diverse eukaryotes. Motifs are generated using \ac{PBM}, SELEX, or ChIP-seq data, but are also mined from other motif resources, such as TRANSFAC. A unique feature of CIS-BP is that the database offers inferred motifs. Based on the similarity of the DBDs, two TFs are binding similar motifs, therefore it is possible to infer a binding profile from one TF to a closely related other TF \cite{weirauch2014determination}.

    Even if current databases contain thousands of motifs from various species, it is important to keep these resources updated by adding new data and refining the old. Finally, new ways of TF-DNA modeling (such as deep-learning models) motivate the expansion or creation of new databases that could offer a variety of TF-DNA interaction models to the community.

    \subsection{Use of TF-DNA binding models}

    Collections of known TF binding profiles can be used in numerous ways. One of the most important applications is the prediction of TF binding sites. Putative TFBSs can be predicted in the entire genome or TF binding enrichment can be determined in a set of regions. Another popular application of the TF motifs is an annotation of \textit{de novo} discovered motifs, which is important when no binding preferences are known for a TF of interest.

      \subsubsection{Prediction of TF binding sites}

      TF-DNA binding models can be used to predict putative transcription factor binding sites in the entire genome or a set of regions of interest (for example, ChIP-seq peaks or promoters) \cite{boeva2016analysis}. When searching promoters, one can find TFBSs for TFs that possibly are regulating that gene. When searching ChIP-seq peaks, possible co-factor binding can be predicted \cite{boeva2016analysis}.

      In TFBS prediction, the true binding sites are expected to score the highest. However, one of the biggest difficulties is setting the PWM score threshold to determine the final set of those TFBS predictions and separate them from the background \cite{boeva2016analysis}. With the higher score cutoff fewer motif instances will be found, and the resulting binding sites are likely to correspond to the strong binding affinity, while with the lower cutoff and more sites detected, the sites might represent both strong and weak binding sites \cite{boeva2016analysis}. Usually, statistical significance (measured through \textit{p}-value) is evaluated to solve the issue of thresholding. For example, computing the probability to observe at least the same number of motif instances with a certain cutoff in a random sequence with total length (equal to the total length of sequences in the set of regions of interest) \cite{boeva2016analysis}. This task becomes difficult computationally if multiple motifs (possibly with overlaps) are being scored, which is relevant when investigating TF cooperativity and co-binding.

      Resources, such as JASPAR, in addition to the collections of PFMs, offer TFBS predictions for the most popular genome assemblies \cite{rauluseviciute2024jaspar}. But also there are various tools designed to perform motif scanning or find over-represented motifs in a set of regions. For example, PWMScan, FIMO, or HOMER \cite{weidemuller2021transcription,grant2011fimo,heinz2010simple}.

      PWMScan is a tool for scanning the whole genome with PWM. The cutoff values can be parameterized as match scores or p-values. The program outputs coordinates of the motif instances with their match scores and p-values \cite{ambrosini2018pwmscan}. FIMO also uses PWMs to scan DNA sequences. It calculates a log-likelihood ratio score for each position in the provided sequences. This score is then converted to a p-value. False discovery rate analysis estimates a q-value for each position in the sequence. The result is a score for each motif instance \cite{grant2011fimo}. HOMER is capable of scanning for the motif across the entire genome but also provides a module to screen for the enrichment of known motifs in a set of regions (for example in ChIP-seq peaks) \cite{heinz2010simple}.

      \subsubsection{Prediction of direct TF binding sites}

      Genome scanning with a motif of interest results in numerous false positives. The resulting predictions of the sites will not be functional \textit{in vivo} even though there is a strong likelihood of TF binding \textit{in vitro}. The phenomenon is known as the futility theorem \cite{wasserman2004applied}. False positively predicted TFBSs are not available for binding due to chromatin structure or local epigenetic properties \cite{mathelier2013next}. It does not mean that such sites are of no importance, but it can be challenging to separate functional TFBSs from non-functional ones.

      Current experimental techniques aim to identify TF binding, but in practice, the data reflect direct and indirect or non-specific TF binding \cite{furey2012chip}. It is challenging to separate the two signals computationally. In addition, while the TFs interact with the DNA through short motifs, the ChIP-seq peaks are usually hundreds of base pairs long. The ChIP-eat computational approach was developed to identify TFBSs that are directly bound by the TF of interest. The method combines ChIP-seq peak data and PWMs to produce sets of direct TFBSs ({Figure \ref{unibind}}) \cite{puig2021unibind,gheorghe2019map}. Given a set of peaks and an optimized PWM of profiled TF, the TFBS enrichment zone is detected. Automatic thresholding identifies the set of TFBSs that are closest to the peak summit and have the highest PWM scores, which is an indication of direct TF-DNA interactions \cite{puig2021unibind,gheorghe2019map}. The resulting TFBSs are of the size of the DNA binding motif. TFBSs from 9 species are provided to the community as a UniBind database \cite{puig2021unibind}. Comparing UniBind TFBSs and predicted TFBSs in the JASPAR database, the conservation of the UniBind ones is much sharper and more concentrated since they are produced using experimental data \cite{puig2021unibind}. But it also shows that the ChIP-eat approach can distinguish functional TFBSs. Therefore, these genomic regions can be a great starting point for further analysis. For example, enrichment analysis can identify, which TFBSs are enriched in a set of regions of interest and which TFs are driving transcription regulation in those regions \cite{puig2021unibind}.

      \subsubsection{Annotating \textit{de novo} discovered motifs}

      Another use of collections of TF binding profiles is an annotation of \textit{de novo} discovered motifs. When unknown motifs are discovered, it can be difficult to interpret them. Therefore, motif comparison can indicate the known motifs from collections that are the most similar to the ones discovered. For example, the most similar motif could be from the same TF family, indicating that the discovered motif is correct.

      There are computational tools designed to compare the motifs of interest against the provided collection of known motifs. For example, TOMTOM compares the query motif against a database of target motifs. It allows for gapped motif-motif alignments, computes the p-value for each motif, and, finally, performs a Bonferroni correction to derive motif E value. TOMTOM integrates a variety of metrics to score similarity (for example, Euclidian distance and Pearson's correlation) \cite{gupta2007quantifying}. RSAT also has a tool, \textit{compare-matrices}, that is used to annotate discovered motifs when the known motif library is provided \cite{thomas2011rsat,santana2022rsat}. The tool can compare two collections of PWMs and, like TOMTOM uses different similarity scoring functions. Multiple or all metrics can be used in comparison and later summarized by computing the mean rank \cite{thomas2011rsat}.

      \subsubsection{Prediction of co-occuring TF binding events}

      As with \textit{de novo} motif discovery, the process of predicting TF binding sites is usually limited to the analysis of each TF binding profile individually, so deciphering the possible co-occurrence of TF motifs requires specialized tools. For example, SpaMo is computational tools that offer the analysis of co-binding events by using a library of known motifs to detect possible co-binding TFs relative to the indicated primary TF. The method was developed to analyze ChIP-seq data, where the profiled TF is the primary TF. It summarizes the spacing and orientation between the primary and secondary motifs and provides the match significance information. The algorithm tests every secondary motif whether there is enriched spacing of the predicted TFBSs with respect to the primary TFBSs \cite{whitington2011inferring}. A great limitation of SpaMo and similar tools is the requirement of known TF binding motif libraries, which means that unknown DNA patterns are missed by default. In addition, the results can return many significant candidates (secondary motifs), which can be difficult to interpret.
