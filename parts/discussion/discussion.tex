\chapter{Discussion}

Transcription factor binding to the DNA is an essential mechanism of transcription regulation. TFs regulate transcription in a time- and space-specific manner. TFs recognize particular short patterns in the DNA, binding motifs. These TF-DNA interaction events result in a regulatory effect. We can analyze DNA patterns recognized by various TFs and explore the gene regulatory mechanisms. However, regulation of transcription is a much more complex process, where multiple transcription factors are involved in dynamic processes. The most commonly used experimental techniques are still limited to analyzing individual TF binding. Massive amounts of such data are available for analysis. ChIP-seq, SELEX, or PBM data enables the building of TF binding models, such as PFMs and PWMs. PWMs are widely used but have limitations that more recent computational approaches try to assess or tackle. Such models' popularity and convenience lead to the development of databases that store them. Computational modeling of individual TFs and the availability of such data and models are all essential for analyzing cooperative TF binding. Computational approaches integrate large amounts of data to decode the interplay between multiple TFs and their binding to the regulatory regions. It is a complex task that has been approached from different angles, and researchers developed several computational tools. Along with the challenge of such tool development, it is vital to carefully represent and interpret the TF cooperativity to learn the biological mechanisms of transcription regulation.

    \section{The current status and future perspectives of computational models for TF-DNA interactions}

    Modeling the TF-DNA interaction is a crucial task in transcription regulation analysis. A long list of different TF binding models exists, and their complexity varies from the simplest ones, such as PWMs, to the most recent deep learning models. One of the most essential uses of TF binding models is predicting TF binding sites. All models are based on a set of assumptions; therefore, they have their advantages and limitations.

    The most widely used model remains to be the simplest one --- PWMs. They are straightforward to use and interpret, and multiple databases (for example, JASPAR) collect and curate collections of PWMs in different species. However, the user of PWMs must consider a few crucial limitations. Firstly, PWM models do not capture dependencies between the residues in the binding site \cite{weirauch2013evaluation}. Each position in the matrix has assumed independence. In the case of DNA methylation, position dependencies are crucial because methylation occurs specifically in the CpG sites. As a result, DNA modifications are usually not represented in most TF-DNA binding models, or their modeling requires a specific approach. Another significant limitation of the PWM models is their inability to represent multiple DNA-binding interfaces for a single TF \cite{weirauch2013evaluation}. In a commonly represented simplified view of transcription regulation, a single TF binds a consensus DNA pattern and affects the regulation of the associated gene. However, in reality, TFs interact with the DNA in multiple ways. For example, TFs can recognize various DNA motifs with weak or strong affinities or bind nonconsensus motifs \cite{slattery2014absence}. In the JASPAR database, profile variants are available for a subset of TFs. Lastly, PWMs cannot model variable width gaps \cite{weirauch2013evaluation}. The matrices are of fixed lengths, but TFs can interact with varying numbers of nucleotides. A typical TF motif derived from computational motif discovery tools has a strong consensus in the middle of the motif and some low informative positions at the edges. In the literature, the positions flanking the main consensus binding site were identified as necessary to binding, specifically regarding the DNA shape-based models or binding of specific TF family members \cite{mathelier2016dna}. However, in the capacity of the PFM models, low IC positions are uninformative and do not impact TFBS predictions but can affect the downstream analysis of the predictions \cite{rauluseviciute2024jaspar}.

    In the COBIND analysis, I used a collection of direct TFBSs from the UniBind database. These TFBSs are derived from ChIP-seq peaks using trained JASPAR TF binding profiles, so TFBSs are of the same lengths as respective TF binding profiles. The motif positions that were not informative are included in the TFBSs by default. COBIND pipeline discovers potential co-binding patterns in the regions flanking input TFBSs. If JASPAR profiles did not have low information positions, the TFBSs would be shorter, and those positions would participate in discovering the co-binding pattern. It is particularly relevant when discovering co-binding patterns with small spacings or partially overlapping co-binding motifs. In the 2024 release of the JASPAR database, we universally applied a trimming algorithm to all the latest versions of TF binding profiles to improve the quality of collections.

    TF binding models are consistently subjected to evaluation. Generally, benchmark studies aim to evaluate the prediction capacity of the models. The first evaluations were comparing predicted TFBSs with annotated binding sites from the literature. In 2013, the DREAM5 consortium aimed to identify how 26 different approaches can classify sequences into bound and unbound using PBM data \cite{weirauch2013evaluation}. They concluded that in most cases, simple PWM models perform similarly to more complex (such as \textit{k}-mer or dinucleotide-based approaches). Furthermore, \textit{in vitro}-derived PWMs accurately reflect the binding \textit{in vivo} \cite{weirauch2013evaluation}. Later, central motif enrichment was introduced as a performance criterion \cite{ambrosini2020insights}. The centrality criterion is also used in data processing for the JASPAR database, where top candidate motifs with the highest centrality values are selected for manual curation. Finally, some benchmark studies evaluated different models, disregarding their annotations. For example, to test how well the models for different TFs in the same TF structural family are predicting the binding sites, demonstrating that family TF binding models can predict the binding of the other family member \cite{ambrosini2020insights}.

    There is still a need to build new TF-DNA interaction models that reflect complex transcription regulation mechanisms. The Ibis challenge (\url{https://ibis.autosome.org/home}) recently was announced. The challenge aims to engage the scientific community in developing and implementing new TF-DNA interaction sequence-level models. Participants will get access to an extensive collection of experimental data on TF-DNA interaction and will build classical PWM models or advanced models, such as deep-learning-based ones.

    Deep learning approaches, such as DeepBind (the first built model for TF-DNA interactions), kick-started a new era in the analysis of TF-DNA interactions. They promise a greater integration of different features that contribute to interactions. In addition, the latest architectures, such as BPNet, demonstrated the capacity to decipher cooperative binding. Currently, no unified resource collects and stores comprehensive lists of trained models. Therefore, there is great potential to train the models using a massive amount of publicly available data, curate them, and store them in a database, such as JASPAR. With the advances in deep learning models, there is a natural expansion of more spartan but widely popular models, such as PWMs.

    \section{Identifying and defining TF co-binding}

    Transcription regulation is a highly complex process involving multiple transcription factors that act together. TFs co-bind (cooperate) or compete for the binding sites at the regulatory regions, such as enhancers. Combinatorial TF binding is essential for the orchestration of spatio-temporal transcription regulation. A few challenges associated with the co-binding of TFs is identifying and defining such events. Limited experimental techniques are available for detecting co-binding DNA sites, so various computational strategies are developed to utilize existing experimental data. However, it is still hard to define and describe how TFs act together in regulatory regions.

    Several TF cooperative binding models were proposed. TFs are bound by strict grammar in the enhanceosome model, while in the billboard model, the grammar is more relaxed. The third model, TF collective, is more variable, where not all TF binding sites might be required, and some TFs bind the region indirectly. All models have support, and some enhancers contain a mixture of binding models \cite{georgakopoulos2023transcription,wang2022towards}. Some studies emphasize the importance of strict TF binding, where TF binding site order and orientation are essential for transcription regulation activity \cite{georgakopoulos2023transcription}. Other researchers conclude that most TFs use a soft syntax, and strict binding rules are not necessary \cite{avsec2021base}. It is important to note that most studies are done on a subset of TFs, often in a specific tissue or cell type. For example, Avsec \textit{et al.} studied the cooperative binding of four pluripotency factors in mouse embryonic stem cells and demonstrated the soft binding syntax. In contrast, Georgakopoulos-Soares \textit{et al.} researched the binding syntax for pair and triplet combinations of eighteen liver-associated TFs and concluded that orientation and order are essential contributors \cite{georgakopoulos2023transcription}. The COBIND approach is limited to discovering co-binding patterns with fixed spacings (enhanceosome model). However, since individual patterns are found as separate components resulting from the non-negative matrix factorization algorithm, the same motif, but with different spacings, can be found if enough sequences are enriched in such combinations.

    An important feature of strictly organized TF binding sites in the enhanceosome model is their high evolutionary conservation. Generally, the regulatory regions are conserved in the genome, indicating the functional importance of these sites across species \cite{berthelot2018complexity}. The regions bound by TFs strongly and effectively are subjected to more significant selective pressure. Therefore, such TF-DNA interactions should be maintained during evolution in related species \cite{stefflova2013cooperativity}. Cooperating TFs are sensitive to the knockouts of their partner TFs. Combinatorial binding is significant for evolutionary stability \cite{stefflova2013cooperativity}. The COBIND project results highlight that the conservation was significantly higher for many found co-binding patterns. Furthermore, with the increased co-binding pattern conservation, a significant increase was also observed at the anchor regions, a subset of original input TFBSs that participate in the co-binding event. The observations of increase in conservation were more frequent than expected by chance.

    Another critical aspect of cooperative binding is cell type specificity and cell fate determination. Combinatorics gives a level of complexity in gene regulation needed for cell differentiation. Therefore, different co-binding events are cell-type specific. For example, the POU5F1 (OCT4) transcription factor co-binds regulatory regions in pluripotent cells with either SOX2 or SOX17 \cite{li2018deconstructing,aksoy2013oct4}. The resulting pair has a particular regulatory effect that defines the fate of the cell. To fully understand the co-binding of TFs, analyzing these events in a cell-type-specific manner would be essential. However, with big data sourced from various open-access resources, cell-type annotations often vary so much that it is hard to find a way to harmonize them for further analysis. A lot of manual effort would be needed to detangle cell-type annotations (more about the importance of database maintenance and update in the following section). Even though in COBIND analysis, systematic cell type analysis was not performed, it was possible to look into individual events and, for example, confirm that POU5F1::SOX2 and POU5F1::SOX17 DNA co-binding patterns were found in pluripotent cells.

    When analyzing DNA sequences for potential co-binding events, the known TF binding models are often used. For example, tools like SpaMo or MCOT rely on libraries of known motifs to investigate the possible cooperativity in a set of sequences \cite{whitington2011inferring,levitsky2019single}. Only using known motifs is a significant limitation, which prevents finding potential new patterns. In the COBIND analysis, a co-binding pattern for CTCF was observed in multiple datasets. The pattern was not highly similar to any known motif. In the literature, it was reported that CTCF has a co-binder, ZBTB3 \cite{wang2020dynamic,boyle2011high}. Later studies showed that the DNA pattern with a fixed spacer from the canonical CTCF binding motif does not represent a binding motif for another protein, but CTCF itself binds the extended motif in a subset of regions \cite{soochit2021ctcf}. Therefore, \textit{de novo} approaches can find more complex co-binding patterns, where not necessarily two TFs bind the region.

    Currently, computational tools for the investigation of cooperativity are based on the analysis of enrichment of known motifs in DNA sequences derived from experimental techniques, such as ChIP-seq or deep learning-based approaches. Generally, to summarize, TF cooperativity can be challenging due to the high complexity of the results. It is complicated to represent all partners for an individual TF, all spacers, and motif orientations. For the deep learning approaches, specialized tools are needed to interpret the models and represent them in a user-friendly way. For example, in a BPNet approach, a tool TF-MoDISco is used to derive the motifs and their distribution syntax from predicted binding sites \cite{avsec2021base,shrikumar2018technical}. MethMotif database provides information for the co-binding status \cite{dyer2024methmotif}. The co-factor evaluation is based on the intersection of two sets of peaks that profile different TFs. For each set of intersected peaks, motifs are generated \textit{de novo} along with the determination of the DNA methylation status \cite{dyer2024methmotif,lin2020tfregulomer}.

    Overall, various studies have attempted to decipher TF co-binding in recent years. Along with developing and updating experimental techniques, improving computational modeling will help further investigate complex cooperation events. 

    \section{The need for maintenance and update of the scientific databases}

    Nowadays, all researchers are using databases. From the well-known scientific literature database PubMed to highly specialized databases, such as JASPAR \cite{canese2013pubmed,rauluseviciute2024jaspar}. Since it is such a trivial everyday activity, many do not think about how these databases come to be and what effort is needed to keep them running. A lot of effort from teams of people is put in to maintain and update scientific databases. For example, the JASPAR database is updated every two years. For two years, an international team of people mines and processes the motif data, then manually curates it while constantly updating data processing pipelines, developing new tools, and maintaining the old ones. Last but not least, in addition to preparing a new release, the JASPAR team is monitoring user feedback, addressing potential errors and bugs in data and tools, and taking suggestions that could be incorporated into the new release.

    One of the most significant aspects of the JASPAR database that distinguishes it from similar databases is that JASPAR relies on manual motif curation. How much manual labor might be needed to maintain the data quality is often underappreciated. Today, much such labor can be eased with various computational tools, but frequently, the final say comes from a researcher. For example, for the JASPAR database update, motifs are prepared using a computational pipeline, and the top three most central motifs from each peak dataset are selected for further inspection \cite{bailey2012inferring}. These motifs can have the same centrality values, so there is no automatic way to choose the best of them. Thus, the curator then evaluates the motifs visually and searches the literature to select the best, if any of them are good at all. Another example of the effort to increase the data quality in databases is revising and refining the classification of the transcription factors. Revising the metadata for current profiles is a separate initiative that might not be considered a priority compared to adding more new data. Since 2022, we put extra effort into updating the metadata of already existing profiles, including searching for orthogonal support for profiles in the UNVALIDATED collection. For the 2024 release, we used a new plant TF classification, Plant-TFClass, to update the metadata for all plant transcription factors in JASPAR \cite{blanc2024plant}. Unfortunately, we have to rely on unsystematic annotations to assign TF classes and families for other taxa, such as nematodes or insects. Notably, much new data was added in the latest release, particularly for these two taxa. This is a great motivation to pay attention to things other than vertebrate taxa in future database updates.

    User input is significant when managing a database. In JASPAR, users can provide feedback using a Q\&A forum. Occasionally, we receive reports on potential bugs, which results in a clarification for the user or the actual fix in the database. In addition, the user questions and feedback can help clarify the database documentation or give ideas on how JASPAR could be improved in the upcoming release. Finally, we provide a specific form and the UNVALIDATED profiles for the users to submit possible validation references. We aim to find orthogonal support for all the UNVALIDATED profiles so that JASPAR users can become part of this. It is vital to provide an attractive environment for users to engage with the database to access and download the data and provide feedback. This helps to ensure the data's quality, accuracy, and relevance.

    Some efforts aim to enhance machine automatic data usability and support its usage by individual researchers. Good data management supports scientific discoveries. \ac{FAIR} serves as a guide for data producers and publishers \cite{wilkinson2016fair}. Briefly, findable means that the data is described with rich metadata, which is clear and identifiable. Accessible means that the data and its metadata can be accessed using a unique identifier, the protocol is open and free, and metadata should be accessible even when the data is no longer available. Interoperability means that the metadata follows FAIR-compatible vocabulary, includes references to other sources, and generally uses formal and broadly applicable language. Finally, reusability implies that data and metadata are released with clear usage license and meet relevant to the community standards \cite{wilkinson2016fair}. These principles should be applied also to the algorithms, tools, and workflows that were used to produce the data. The authors of FAIR emphasize that beyond high-quality data production, it is important to include a notion of "long-term care" of digital assets. The care of such assets includes ethical considerations for personal data. Specific guidelines for sensitive data are created and should be followed. In the JASPAR database, FAIR principles are followed. The open-access database provides the data in conventional formats, such as JASPAR, MEME, TRANSFAC, and RAW-formatted frequency matrices for TF binding. Motif instances are provided in well-known BED and FASTA formats. The metadata associated with each profile is provided whenever possible. However, it is important to note that the amount of the metadata highly depends on its availability in the data source. All this data can be accessed in various ways: user-friendly website, Python and Bioconductor packages, and API. Users can choose the most comfortable access to them. The results generated by the COBIND pipeline are also freely available as a user-friendly interactive webpage. Users can browse the page directly and download associated data.

    Data accessibility is essential to the research \cite{tedersoo2021data}. However, open access is not commonly accepted. Some researchers would even consider computational biologists, who use published experimental data as "research parasites" \cite{longo2016data}. The bioinformatics community actively promotes open data sharing since the development of the field. A couple of the best examples of data sharing are the Human Genome Project and the ENCODE project \cite{international2001initial,encode2012integrated}. Without data sharing, a lot of expensive experimental data would remain understudied. Several repositories, such as ZENODO and Figshare, store and retrieve extensive data. Importantly, these repositories assign the \ac{DOI}, allowing the proper crediting of the producers of that data \cite{zenodo,singh2011figshare}. With computational methods evolving, the already produced data can be analyzed again or differently to give new scientific insights. For example, DNase I hypersensitivity data from ENCODE was used by Vierstra \textit{et al.} to compute the DHS footprints \cite{vierstra2020global}. Since ENCODE data was also used in UniBind, I could reuse the footprints to analyze the co-binding patterns in footprints in matching cell types for COBIND. However, the FAIR principles are essential to clarify the metadata to ensure this reusability. Cell types were matched successfully because the data and metadata came from the same original resource, ENCODE, and were well-preserved in the subsequent studies. Summarizing cell-type information for data that comes from many sources is still an arduous task. Some resources, such as BRENDA \cite{gremse2010brenda}, provide tissue ontologies, but to apply it to the metadata would still require manual input.

    To be able to find the data, access it, use it to develop tools, and then produce more data while navigating and entangling complex biological questions --- that is the job of a bioinformatician. Every researcher should aim to be able to do it freely and then make it available to fellow researchers. Scientific databases are an essential part of a researcher's life. Well-used databases must be updated and maintained to ensure the research progress.

    \section{Development of new bioinformatics tools}

    Besides using scientific databases, researchers use computational tools. Some of these tools are commercial and come with laboratory equipment, but many are developed by individual bioinformaticians or small groups of researchers. The latter category of tools can exist as command line tools, packages (Bioconductor, R, Python), or pipelines (Nextflow, Snakemake) \cite{gentleman2004bioconductor,r2023language,python2009language,di2017nextflow,molder2021sustainable}. The quality of this software varies from extremely easy to use to unusable, unmaintained tools. The lack of software quality in bioinformatics can hinder research progress because a lot of time is used to debug or adapt existing tools. Certain aspects of software quality should be followed whenever a new bioinformatics tool is being developed.

    The scientific code should always be organized to make it easier to use. It could be organized into a package or a pipeline. For example, COBIND has quite a simple organization. It is written as a collection of scripts, but all are organized into a Snakemake pipeline, which is parameterized using a configuration file. JASPAR is much more complex from a software perspective. The database update relies on many individual software pieces. In 2024, we summarized them into three main software components, "3D", organized into pipelines. (1) The "\textbf{D}iscovery" pipeline in Snakemake discovers motifs from peak data or processes external motifs and prepares them for manual curation. (2) The "\textbf{D}ownstream" pipeline (now also implemented in Snakemake) collects curated motifs and prepares all additional results and data needed for the website. (3) The "\textbf{D}eployment" pipeline, now organized as an Ansible playbook and launched using the Jenkins platform, takes care of creating the updated website. The first and second components are intercepted by manual curation, while the second and third are separated by data collection and moving into an appropriate place. Even with the manual curation step, connecting all the components needed to perform a complex task, such as a database update, is possible. Organizing scientific software into pipelines is not the only solution; for the user, pipelines are relatively easy to understand and follow.

    Developing a pipeline or a package can be a personal choice if the software is usable. One of the biggest challenges when reusing someone else's written software is dependency management. Therefore, dependency management should be one of the priorities when developing a new tool. It is essential to provide dependency requirements in the tool documentation. Several automated ways exist to manage dependencies and ensure the tool will work as intended. Virtual environments isolate tool dependencies from a global environment, ensuring that appropriate packages are present and will be used by the tool. One of the most popular tools for virtual environment management is Conda \cite{anaconda}. The more recent and great way to isolate the entire software is by building containers. Containers can isolate everything from the code to the system libraries and settings. From the user's perspective, downloading the container is enough, and none of the dependencies need to be installed besides the tool that runs the container. The most widely used containerization tools in bioinformatics are Singularity and Docker \cite{kurtzer2017singularity,merkel2014docker}. Singularity containers can be integrated into Snakemake workflows, which makes those pipelines very convenient. Built containers can be placed into repositories, such as Docker Hub (\url{https://hub.docker.com/}) for open access and version control. COBIND and JASPAR software are containerized using Docker (\url{https://hub.docker.com/repositories/cbgr}). Since JASPAR consists of multiple software components, several containers are built and combined in the different Snakemake pipelines. COBIND container provides the necessary environment to run the pipeline.

    Finally, an essential aspect of scientific software quality is proper version control, accessibility, and code documentation. This should be applied to bioinformatics tools and all scientific codes written for data analysis. It is the only way to ensure the transparency and reproducibility of the published results. Tools, such as Bitbucket (\url{https://bitbucket.org/}) or GitHub (\url{https://github.com/}) provide all functionalities for code storage, the control of version and releases, and documentation. These practices are helpful not only to potential users but also to bioinformaticians themselves. Using branches and writing appropriate commit messages will help when developing the code. Proper README documentation should inform what the software is about, the usage, the required and optional inputs, and the expected output. JASPAR and COBIND software are freely available and extensively documented on Bitbucket.

    Following sound software development principles gives users confidence and motivates them to use the tools. With the reproducibility crisis in science \cite{baker20161}, it is imperative to invest time in the development and maintenance of high-quality, user-friendly software.
